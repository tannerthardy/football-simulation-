### An advanced field manual for running-back vision, biomechanics, run-game reads, contact, protection, and receiving

**Volume V | Football Mechanics & Simulation Series**  
**October 2026**

> **Central thesis:** A running back does not run toward a static hole. He manipulates defenders with his track, interprets leverage as blockers and defenders move, selects a time-sensitive opening, and executes a physically constrained path through traffic. The best backs do this with different styles, but all must connect *eyes, feet, timing, shoulders, and decisions*.

## PART I — How a running back sees the field

### 1. The run is a moving problem, not a predetermined lane

Every rushing attempt has three simultaneous geometries. **Scheme geometry** defines intended blocking surfaces, combinations, pullers, and possible entry points. **Contact geometry** describes where offensive and defensive bodies actually are, including their momentum, leverage, hand control, and direction. **Pursuit geometry** describes when a defender from the second or third level can arrive at a lane or tackle point. A runner must continually reconcile the three. A visually open gap may close before he reaches it; a tight crease may become viable if his track keeps a linebacker on the wrong side of a block.

A useful decision sequence is **identify the picture → press the proper track → read the controlling defender or block → decide → get vertical → solve the next defender**. This sequence is conceptual, not a fixed timer. Better runners often anticipate that a defender is about to be displaced while average runners wait until open space is obvious. Poor anticipation can also create an unnecessary tackle for loss when the imagined opening never develops.

Distinguish **designed aiming point**, **initial track**, **decision point**, **final entry gap**, and **ball-carrier path**. These are not the same thing. On wide zone, the back can run a wide track to make defenders pursue laterally, then cut vertically through an interior gap. On duo, a back may press behind a double team to manipulate a linebacker, then enter beside the double. Judging whether he “hit the hole” without knowing his prescribed track confuses a successful read with freelancing.

**A gap** lies between center and guard; **B** between guard and tackle; **C** outside tackle (or between tackle and attached tight end); **D** outside an attached tight end. Gap labels are useful for analysis, but players often key a **defender's leverage or a block's hip**, not a painted channel. The gap itself can migrate several feet during the snap.

| Variable | What the runner needs to determine | Frequent mistake |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Entry landmark | Where to press to preserve the scheme | Looking for daylight and abandoning track immediately |
| First-level read | What an identified DL/block is doing | Watching the ball-side shoulder only |
| Second-level key | Who can plug, scrape, or overrun | Looking exclusively at the line |
| Edge control | Whether force/contain is sealed or available | Bouncing into an unblocked force player |
| Backside integrity | Whether pursuit or a read defender remains | Cutting behind a blocker straight into contain |
| Arrival time | Whether the lane survives until RB arrival | Cutting toward a temporarily open but disappearing gap |

### 2. What the back reads before the snap

Good runners start by understanding the **call**, not by scanning randomly for the largest pre-snap hole. They know the intended surface, backfield alignment, number of possible blockers, unblocked defenders, and how the quarterback's mesh determines timing. They also know whether a puller, tight end, receiver, or second-level combination is the crucial moving landmark.

The pre-snap defensive picture includes down-lineman technique (0, 1, 2i, 3, 4i, 5, wide 9), number of box players, linebacker depth and alignment, front strength and overhang location, the presence of a safety fitted near the box, and the likely force/contain player. An even front can become an odd-surface problem after a slant. A safety may be unavailable to fit the run if his coverage assignment requires carrying a vertical release. Motion can create a new nickel or safety fitter late. None of this grants the runner knowledge of the true defensive call.

A good pre-snap plan consists of **primary picture plus alternatives**. Example: “Inside zone to the right; 3-technique is playside; center/guard may combine backside; if playside defender crosses the guard's face, expect a tighter or backside solution; if linebacker overflows, look behind the movement.” This plan must remain revisable. Defensive movement, penetration, and the response of the combinations are discovered after the snap.

**Front recognition is probabilistic.** A mugged linebacker may drop. A nose can lag across the center. A 4i can squeeze to cancel the B gap or spike outside. A runner who assumes the drawn gap remains valid after movement is likely to collide with the front rather than exploit its displacement.

### 3. Eyes: what to focus on and what to see peripherally

“Vision” isn't a single ability. It includes target selection, spatial awareness, working memory, peripheral tracking, anticipation, and the speed with which visual evidence turns into action. A runner usually doesn't have time to inspect every defender serially. His initial focus should correspond to the coaching key; the rest is monitored in peripheral vision and through expected patterns.

The **first visual layer** is the defender or blocking relationship that controls whether the called lane survives. The **second** is the fitting linebacker or force player whose reaction may decide the back's eventual cut. The **third** is the pursuing safety or backside defender that controls the gain after the line. Good coaching drills deliberately expose the back to a different first-level and second-level picture on repeated reps, not the same gap opening every time. The Ole Miss coaching material emphasizes that the back sees a different first- and second-level picture every snap and ties eye training to tracks and landmarks [S1].

For analysis, separate **detection** (the runner saw movement), **interpretation** (he recognized the likely lane consequence), **commitment** (he selected an entry), and **execution** (he physically performed the cut). A runner can choose correctly but fail to execute because he is too upright, crossing his feet, or has already oversped the landmark. Conversely, athletic improvisation can rescue a wrong read and still earn the wrong process grade.

### 4. Alignment, cadence, mesh, and the first three steps

A half-yard of depth can change a run. Backs in pistol, sidecar shotgun, offset gun, singleback under center, and an I-formation reach the point of attack with different paths, ball-handling constraints, and opportunities to read the line. A shotgun mesh may force the RB to arrive beside the QB with square shoulders while the QB rides the ball. Under center, the RB can already be accelerating downhill when the handoff is transferred, but his vision of the line may be obscured by the QB and other bodies.

The stance should allow a decisive first step without telegraphing direction. A back's early footwork might use an open/crossover sequence, a lead step, a lateral shuffle, or a counter step, depending on the install. Evaluate it by outcomes: **does he maintain the designated track, receive the ball cleanly, keep the shoulders available to the required cuts, and arrive at the read at the correct tempo?** “No false steps” is too crude if a controlled step serves a meaningful timing or alignment function.

Mesh security is a timed partnership. The QB presents the ball in a consistent location; the RB maintains a consistent belly/near-arm pocket and clamps the ball on schedule. With read-option or RPO, the back must not interpret the QB's indecision as permission to change the designed path. In a slow-mesh system, the RB may stay attached to the QB longer; that changes both the visual picture and acceleration profile.

A coaching checkpoint at the first three steps: (1) the back hasn't drifted off his landmark; (2) eyes are on the proper key; (3) torso/head posture permits vision and upcoming redirection; (4) ball security is already being prepared; and (5) his velocity matches the blocking development rather than blindly maximizing speed.

### 5. Running mechanics: center of mass, gait, and productive cuts

Running between linemen is not just sprinting at reduced speed. It is a sequence of controlled accelerations, narrow-path navigation, contact avoidance, and rapid redirections. The hips and center of mass should generally be under control so a plant can occur without a lengthy braking phase. **Running low** does not mean bending excessively at the waist and looking at the ground: excessive forward fold can restrict visual information, step placement, and arm freedom. Good forward lean is produced through useful body position and ground-force direction, not just head-down posture.

**Stride length is situational.** Longer strides can cover space well in the open field, but a back arriving at a tight crease with an overextended step is poorly positioned to change direction. Shorter, faster, controlled steps near the read can preserve options. There is also a cost: excessive chopping of the feet can slow arrival enough for the hole to disappear. Good backs modulate stride *before* they reach the decision point.

A **one-foot cut** uses a decisive loaded step to redirect and accelerate. A **jump cut** temporarily removes normal ground support, shifts laterally, then lands and reaccelerates. The jump cut can create a dramatic lateral lane change but carries airborne vulnerability and can waste time when a simple plant would suffice. A Kansas coaching presentation specifically favors teaching a one-foot “wind back” in situations where players are tempted to jump cut by default [S2]. A **dead-leg or hesitation move** manipulates the defender's expectation with asymmetric loading and pace; it fails when performed too far from the defender or when the runner cannot accelerate afterward.

**Get skinny** means reducing the effective width of the running profile through posture, shoulder orientation, arm carriage, and foot placement when crossing a tight crease; it does not mean becoming collision-free. A runner entering a narrow lane can rotate his shoulders slightly to pass through without losing the downhill path, but rotation that opens the torso too far also reduces ability to absorb a square hit. The optimum depends on lane width, contact angle, and projected defender arrival.

| Technique | Best mechanical use | Cost or vulnerability |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Controlled one-foot cut | Fast, efficient directional redirection | Requires prepared plant and traction |
| Jump cut | Large lateral adjustment across a quickly closing lane | Airborne, slower reacceleration, landing risk |
| Stutter/hesitation | Freezes a second-level defender or changes pursuit timing | Consumes time; can invite penetration |
| Dip-and-rip through crease | Shrinks exposure, protects ball, passes glancing contact | Limits immediate vision/hand freedom |
| Speed cut/arc | Redirects with limited braking in space | Requires larger radius and available grass |
| Stop-start | Punishes defender's overcommitment | Loses momentum against disciplined pursuit |

### 6. Tempo: when patience helps and when it hurts

Patience is frequently misdescribed as moving slowly. The better concept is **control of arrival relative to blocking development**. A back can take a restrained initial track, force linebackers to declare, then explode when the runner-blocker-defender relationship resolves. “Press and burst” is useful only if the back reaches the correct landmark before the defense resets.

There are at least four tempos: **mesh tempo** (arrive at QB safely and on schedule), **press tempo** (stress the defender while a combination develops), **decision tempo** (short period of high choice availability), and **vertical tempo** (accelerate through the selected gap). Coaches may use different labels. A back who runs full speed into the heels of his linemen has not necessarily shown decisiveness; he may have destroyed the possibility of a successful second-level block. A back who delays at the line while a linebacker closes an available crease hasn't shown patience either.

A useful coaching question is: **Did the RB force a defender to make the first commitment?** Pressing the appropriate line can hold the backside linebacker in place, stretch the playside defender toward a reach block, or put the extra fitter behind a blocker. A constant-speed running back cannot intentionally create those advantages.

### 7. Reading blocking leverage inside a crowd

The runner should look for **relationships**, not just isolated jerseys. Which hip of the guard is exposed? Has a defensive tackle been reached or is he crossing face? Is a combination block secure enough for one lineman to climb? Is the linebacker fitting over the top, filling downhill, or scraping backside? Did a puller kick the edge outward, or did the edge wrong-arm and spill the play? Those relationships forecast future space.

Treat a lane as having at least three dimensions: **entry width** (can shoulders/ball fit?), **survival time** (will defenders close before the runner arrives?), and **exit direction** (is there an immediate second-level tackle?). A large hole with a clean unblocked safety three yards beyond it may be less valuable than a tighter crease with favorable leverage against the safety.

When a runner is “behind the blocker,” the key is often the **blocker's near hip and the defender's position relative to it**. Hugging the near hip can keep a defender attached to the blocker and reduce the direct tackling line. Running too far behind turns the RB into the lineman's heels; running too wide lets the defender fall off the block. The correct spacing evolves dynamically and differs between zone, lead, and puller concepts.

## PART II — Reading the major run families

### 8. Inside zone: press, diagnose, bang, bend, bounce

Inside zone is a family of schemes built on covered/uncovered combinations and displacement according to the offense's rules. The back normally presses a defined interior landmark and reads front movement while preserving multiple gap options. Some staffs use the playside guard's near hip; others teach the crack of the center or another track. One Ole Miss example teaches “press the crack of the center,” controlled tempo, square shoulders, and pressure on the line [S1]. **No single landmark should be treated as universal.**

The classic shorthand **bang, bend, bounce** describes a set of possible finishes, not permission to make three equally attractive random decisions. **Bang:** take a workable playside/downhill seam. **Bend:** redirect behind defensive overreaction into a viable backside seam. **Bounce:** escape outside when leverage and force structure genuinely permit it; reckless bouncing commonly bypasses the most favorable blocking angles.

A useful read begins by locating the assigned down-lineman or combination relative to the track. If the defender is displaced outside, the interior crease may be useful. If he penetrates or crosses face, the RB adjusts to the block's other hip or behind movement. Then check the linebacker's declared fit. A back should not cut *because* a linebacker moves—he should cut because that movement creates a lane he can reach ahead of the rest of the fit.

**Mechanically:** arrive with square-enough shoulders to preserve frontside and backside options; use tempo to make the linebacker's fit declare; choose one efficient plant; accelerate vertically. Keep the ball high and tight and be prepared for the cutback defender immediately after entry. The move from track to cut should be small enough that the back doesn't cross several gap columns on a slow lateral path.

| Post-snap picture | RB interpretation | Efficient response |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Playside combo gains displacement; LB displaced outside | Interior lane is opening | Press, plant, bang downhill |
| Playside penetration eliminates intended entry | Frontside track contaminated | Tighten, bend behind flow if backside secure |
| Nose lags across center into backside A | Backside not clean despite visual daylight | Avoid blind cutback; use another controlled crease |
| Front slants playside; scrape backer replaces | Apparent gap may be an exchange trap | Track scrape backer, avoid slant-created void if filled |
| Wide defensive overrun but edge sealed | Possibility of bounce | Only bounce if force and pursuit angles allow |

### 9. Tight zone and split zone: different geometry, related eyes

Tight zone generally emphasizes a downhill interior entry and may be paired with a backside tight end/H-back slice that handles the edge or read defender. The exact blocking surface varies by offense. The RB cannot treat the slice as proof that the backside is open; the H-back can be late, wrong-shouldered, or redirected by the edge. A linebacker may follow the H-back or fit against the initial flow, creating a new opening or a false clue.

Against a **Tite/Mint front**, two 4i defenders can constrict the B gaps and make interior combinations behave differently than they do against a standard Over front. The back may need to be more patient about the nose's behavior and quicker to recognize which gap a scraping linebacker will occupy. A nose that controls the center can prevent a clean vertical crease, but a well-timed combination can create a narrow seam between constrained front players.

In **split zone**, backside H-back travel can influence the edge. If the edge widens to meet the slice, space may exist inside. If he wrong-arms and spills the action, the ball may be redirected; if he squeezes while the slice gets a secure seal, the inside path can develop. Some schemes treat this as a designed read of the edge; others require the back to read the interior combination and merely account for backside security. Install-specific rules override generic labels.

### 10. Outside zone and wide zone: stretch a defense to cut vertically

Outside/wide zone often looks like a sprint toward the sideline, but high-level coaching frequently treats it as a **lateral stress that creates vertical entry**. Aiming points vary: outside leg of tackle, tight-end landmark, or another surface. The back must press the correct landmark far enough to stretch linebackers and force a reach-block decision; turning up too soon can leave the defense stacked. Continuing laterally after the entry has opened can lead straight into the pursuit arc.

**Bounce/bang/bend** is a useful outside-zone shorthand, but its definitions vary among staffs. In a common interpretation: bounce if the edge is secured and the alley is available; bang vertically when a frontside crease appears; bend behind pursuit if defenders overrun and backside pursuit is controlled. The RB should use the coached read defender/blocker sequence, not literal gap labels alone. USA Football's outside-zone vision guidance emphasizes teaching responses to specific block outcomes rather than telling backs to simply find a hole [S3].

The crucial interaction is **press versus overrun**. If the back maintains his aiming point, the force defender or play-side linebacker may move laterally to contain. The frontside blocker can then seal or run that defender outward, allowing a plant off the blocker's inside hip. If the RB shortcuts the track, defenders may never widen; the RB has made the block harder for his own offensive line.

An ideal zone cut minimizes wasted lateral displacement once the read declares. The back plants and gets north-south as rapidly as the geometry permits. A runner who can cut on a single loaded step preserves speed; a runner who must hop laterally, gather, and reaccelerate grants time to backside pursuit. **Outside zone is not automatically a race to the sideline** [S3, S4].

### 11. Duo: vertical doubles and linebacker manipulation

Duo is commonly taught as a downhill, no-puller gap-style concept with multiple vertical double teams. It can resemble inside zone at the snap, yet its intended displacement and linebacker read are often distinct. A common teaching point is to press near a guard's hip and read a designated linebacker—often the Mike or a scheme-specific point linebacker—whose fit determines which side of the double team becomes advantageous [S5, S6]. This is an example of why “look for the hole” is not enough.

**If the key linebacker overflows outside the intended vertical track**, the RB may press and insert underneath him. **If he plugs downhill**, the RB can bend around the leverage of the double or attack another defined outlet, but only if the outside defender and backside pursuit permit it. There is substantial coaching variation: some staffs use two linebacker keys or a minus-one fitter; their progressions are not interchangeable [S6]. A simulation must represent the actual installation, not invent a universal Mike-read formula.

Tempo matters because the first-level doubles need time to gain vertical movement. The runner's track should keep the linebacker from confidently cheating to the final gap. If the back declares the final cut too early, he can release the linebacker from the double-team relationship. If he waits until the double breaks down, he gets tackled at the mesh point of the block.

Compare two plays: on inside zone, a guard might be trying to overtake a defender laterally and give the back a cut relative to the DL's movement. On duo, the guard/tackle may be trying to displace the defender vertically while climbing to a linebacker. The back's pressing behavior should reflect which relationship must develop.

![Duo: make the fitted linebacker choose a side of the double](figures/06_duo.svg)

### 12. Power: following a puller without following him blindly

Traditional Power often includes playside down blocks, a backside guard pulling for a play-side linebacker, and a kick-out or edge-control block by another player. But there are many Power families (one-back, two-back, insert, read/option, GT variations). The RB must know **which blocker is the insert/puller, who sets the edge, and which gap is the intended first threat**.

A typical coaching target is to press downhill toward the call-side interior/off-tackle landmark, allow the kick-out and insert blocks to form, and follow the **insert blocker's inside hip** through the hole. The runner should not follow the pulling guard literally if doing so leads him into the defender the guard is attacking. He needs to evaluate the relationship between puller and linebacker: does the puller win inside leverage, wash the fitter outward, or get crossed up?

A wrong-arm edge can spill the kick-out, changing the intended track. The RB's answer depends on how the scheme coaches the play: fold vertically off the edge's displacement, follow a log around the spill, or redirect outside to another blocker. A runner who bounces before the edge declares can create a loss even when the scheme would have hit inside.

**Timing checkpoint:** if the runner overtakes his puller he becomes an unblocked player in the hole. If he sits flat-footed behind the puller, backside pursuit catches him. Skilled Power runners match cadence to the pulling block, then accelerate as the second-level defender commits.

### 13. Counter: let misdirection work without becoming late

Counter can feature GT (guard/tackle), GH (guard/H-back), or other puller combinations. A counter step or slow mesh can pull second-level defenders away from the actual entry. The back must sell enough initial flow to create a false step, but not such a long detour that the pullers arrive without him or the intended gap collapses.

For a common GT Counter, the first puller threatens a kick-out or log on the edge, and the second wraps to the second level. The runner keys the first puller's **leverage outcome** and follows the second puller's **insertion relationship**, but in practice the two events occur quickly and overlap. If the edge spills the first block, the runner may be redirected toward the wrap/outside lane depending on the offensive rules. If the edge boxes and kick-out succeeds, a vertical lane may form inside the kick-out.

The critical mistake is **running to where the blocker is now rather than where the block will create space**. An advanced runner anticipates the blocker's momentum and the defender's movement: if the puller is widening the edge, he can enter just inside it. If the edge defeats the kick-out and sits in the hole, continuing into the block produces a predictable collision.

Jet/orbit motion can improve the counter picture by pulling eyes and adjusting coverage-based run support, but it doesn't guarantee a false step. A disciplined linebacker may follow his guard key and never leave the counter fit. The RB must react to what actually happened, not simply assume the deception worked.

### 14. Trap, wham, and influence: attacking a defender's aggression

Trap and wham concepts invite a down lineman upfield, then strike him from an unexpected blocking angle using a puller or H-back. The RB's job is to understand that the opening can form **behind the penetrator**, not at the spot he occupied pre-snap. An overeager runner may collide with the penetrator before the trapper arrives.

Read the penetrating defender's depth and angle, then the trap block's contact. If the defender continues upfield past the runner's track and the trapper arrives, a sharp vertical crease can form. If the defender recognizes the trap, squeezes, or collisions disrupt the trapper, the back may need a tight cut. These schemes have less patience available once contact occurs: the surprise angle is the advantage, and a delayed runner allows other defenders to recover.

A wham block by an H-back or wing can similarly create an interior displacement. The RB must avoid drifting toward the wham block simply because the impact looks dramatic; the correct path may be the blocker’s near hip or the space he has vacated.

### 15. Pin-and-pull, toss, sweep, and perimeter schemes

Pin-and-pull can have covered linemen pin defenders inside while uncovered linemen pull toward force and alley defenders. The runner must assess the **perimeter's edge control** and whether the first puller seals or logs the force. On toss, pitch timing and ball security are additional priorities; a lateral ball in flight cannot be treated as a completed handoff.

For outside plays, the RB may have to **stretch long enough to secure the edge** without running laterally into a defender with superior pursuit speed. The decision can be to turn up inside a pulling tackle, continue outside a sealed force player, or cut back as over-pursuit opens a seam. In wide space, the best line isn't always the nearest sideline: minimizing the pursuit defender's interception opportunity is often more valuable than maximizing apparent lateral space.

Jet sweeps and fly motions bring a very different handoff clock and can make a receiver the runner. The same principles still apply: protect the transfer, identify force, manipulate pursuit, and get vertical at the first advantageous angle. But acceleration state and alignment are different from a stationary tailback on toss.

### 16. Draws, delays, and the deceptive run clock

On a draw, the offense initially suggests pass. The back may show protection or a delayed route before turning downhill. Unlike zone or Power, a significant part of the scheme's value comes from **defensive rush depth**: pass rushers are moved away from their run gaps, and linebackers may retreat or hesitate.

The RB must understand the draw's handoff and route to the gap; too early a downhill movement telegraphs the run and collapses the pass-rush illusion. Too late lets the defense retrace to the line. The visual key is often the depth and interior separation of rushers plus the linebacker’s initial drop/trigger. A draw can be especially effective when defenders are rushing aggressively, but a disciplined front can still constrict the intended lane.

Delayed screens and slip releases share the deception principle but introduce eligibility, blocking-timing, and forward-pass constraints. The RB should never assume every apparent pass rusher has abandoned run responsibility.

### 17. Zone read, split-flow options, RPO mesh, and quarterback run conflicts

In many zone-read structures, the offense intentionally leaves a defensive player unblocked and gives the **QB** the give/keep read; the RB normally runs his prescribed track and secures the mesh. The RB is not allowed to independently cut toward the defender who has been assigned to the QB's read. If the defense scrape-exchanges—edge crashes toward the RB while linebacker scrapes for QB—both the QB and the RB experience a different downstream picture from a standard read.

On inverted veer/Bash structures, the RB may be on the sweep track while the QB threatens the inside run. The RB's eye and footwork rules differ substantially from inside zone because his intended attack is the perimeter and the exchanged defender may control the bounce. On an RPO, the RB must consistently execute the run track even when the quarterback pulls to pass; a poor mesh or drifting path can interfere with passing timing and line blocking.

Pistol and option systems shift the QB/RB relationship, especially when the RB crosses the QB’s face or executes an option pitch phase. If the pitch becomes possible, the back's track, spacing, and hands must be synchronized with quarterback leverage and pitch timing. An offense cannot model these details as ordinary inside-zone footwork with a different animation.

## PART III — Operating between the linemen and creating yards

### 18. Setting up blocks: the back as an active participant

A runner is not merely the beneficiary of blocking. His path influences what the blocker can accomplish. A defender who must account for an apparent frontside run cannot immediately pursue a backside cut without conceding leverage. An LB who commits to fill a gap can be screened by an offensive lineman climbing to him. A safety who closes a wide alley may be attacked inside if the RB changes the aiming angle before the safety reaches his tackle point.

**Pressing is manipulation.** The runner approaches a landmark that makes a defender honor one threat, then cuts as that defender commits. Pressing the frontside A/B surface may hold the backside LB, allowing a blocker to overtake. Pressing the outer hip of a reach block may persuade a defender to fight outside and expose the crease inside. The runner needs to remain physically capable of reaching the lane when the defender finally commits; pressing with excessive speed and closed hips removes his own options.

**Hug the block without running into it.** Against a linebacker engaged with a climbing guard, the runner may travel close enough to the guard’s near hip that the linebacker cannot fall off either side cleanly. The RB then wins on the proper side of the blocker's leverage. The wrong version follows directly into the guard's back, stopping the runner's acceleration and allowing other defenders to arrive.

**Manipulate with pace, not only direction.** A small hesitation can freeze an LB just long enough for a combo blocker to climb; a sudden acceleration can cross the line before an overhang arrives. Different defensive archetypes respond differently. A downhill trigger linebacker may attack the first opening; a patient shuffle linebacker may wait for the runner to declare and require the RB to choose another manipulation strategy.

### 19. Second-level decision-making: from daylight to pursuit angles

Once through the first crease, the runner shifts from gap selection to defender manipulation. Rather than look for another abstract hole, identify the **near defender, his leverage, and the angle of the next helper**. A safety closing from outside-in may be vulnerable to an inside cut; a backside linebacker closing inside-out may force the runner toward the boundary. A successful open-field move should change the defender's feasible interception path, not simply alter the animation on the runner.

Approach angle matters. Running directly at a defender can keep two exit paths alive, but it can also eliminate time to react if the defender has superior balance and support. Attacking a defender's outside shoulder may force him to open his hips, setting up an inside plant. Against disciplined leverage, the best choice may be to take the protected path for guaranteed positive yards instead of attempting a low-probability long cut.

**When to use a move:** the defender is near enough that he must react, but far enough that the runner can complete the plant and accelerate before contact. An early juke gives away the destination; a late juke occurs after the defender has already reached tackle range. This window varies with both players' velocities and foot-load states. A quick back can exploit tighter timing windows than a long-striding power back, but the latter may prefer to choose a physical contact angle.

### 20. Contact balance: collision management rather than a magic trait

Contact balance is the ability to continue useful movement after an external force changes momentum or posture. It is not equivalent to weight or strength. It combines pre-contact balance, base width, foot placement, trunk stiffness/control, anticipation, contact point relative to the center of mass, and the speed of a recovery step. A lighter runner can survive a glancing blow by avoiding the defender's center; a heavy runner can fall if hit across a planted leg while airborne or rotated.

The most important distinction is **square and direct contact versus glancing and rotational contact**. A defender striking square through the RB's mass can abruptly reduce forward momentum. A side strike farther from the runner's center can create yaw and tipping; the back may recover by replanting away from the force. A high contact point can rotate the torso backward; low contact can threaten the base and leg drive. There is no single contact-outcome roll that reproduces all these relationships.

Coaching language like “run behind your pads” should not become “lower your head.” Maintain safe head positioning, keep vision available, and drive with legs and hips rather than leading with the helmet. Rules on impermissible use of the helmet apply, and collision simulation should never require unsafe head-first posture [S8]. A **finish** can mean running through a glancing arm tackle, staying on the feet after shoulder contact, or falling forward under control in a short-yardage situation—different techniques are appropriate to each.

Three useful subskills: **contact anticipation** (brace and organize before impact), **contact reduction** (turn a clean tackle into partial contact through angle), and **contact recovery** (replant, regain balance, and accelerate). Model these separately. Some backs are outstanding at avoiding direct contact but ordinary when they are struck square. Others can absorb heavy contact but rarely make the first defender miss.

### 21. Hand fighting, stiff arms, spins, and defender manipulation

A runner's free hand can protect his space, deny a defender access to the torso, and modify a would-be tackle. But that same hand is also needed for ball security or rapid balance correction. A stiff arm works best when the runner maintains a favorable position against the tackler's approach; reaching too early or too far can turn the shoulder, expose the ball, and slow acceleration.

A **stiff arm** usually manages separation by contacting an available upper-body region while the runner continues moving through a safe angle. It should not be represented as a guaranteed knockback force. The defender may slip beneath it, strike the extending arm, redirect toward the runner's near hip, or wrap after the first arm contact. Its value may be simply delaying a wrap by a fraction of a second.

A **spin** requires the runner to enter the move with the correct body orientation and rotational opportunity. It can defeat an angled tackler who commits to a shoulder, but it tends to sacrifice direct visual access and ball exposure unless the ball is tightly controlled. The spin should have finite rotation time, translational momentum cost, and a landing/reacceleration phase. A jump-cut-to-spin combination is possible but mechanically expensive.

**Shoulder manipulation** is often more subtle than a highlight move: the runner turns his near shoulder away from the defender's strike line to make the contact glancing, then continues through the crease. This is distinct from the unsafe practice of lowering the head into a defender. Against arm tackles, a compact ball carriage, aggressive free arm, and continued leg drive can prevent a clean wrap without a spectacular animation.

### 22. Running styles are clusters of decisions, not one trait

Different backs can be effective within the same scheme while making very different decisions. A runner's style is an emergent pattern in **landmark discipline, tempo, preferred cut size, risk appetite, contact choice, footwork efficiency, and willingness to trade immediate yardage for later opportunity**. One label never completely captures a player.

| Useful archetype | What stands out | Best conditions | Risk profile |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| One-cut zone specialist | Efficient vertical transition from lateral press | Outside/inside zone with flowing linebackers | May miss nonlinear opportunities requiring second adjustment |
| Patient press-and-read back | Uses pace to move linebackers and exploit doubles | Duo, wide zone, combinations developing late | Can wait too long against penetration |
| Downhill crease hitter | Early decisive entry, compact stride, strong acceleration | Tight zone, Power, favorable interior gap | Can miss backside openings or run into traffic |
| Lateral creator | Exceptional stop-start, jump-cut, tight-space evasion | Dirty blocking pictures, loose pursuit fits | Extra moves can waste time and invite pursuit |
| Contact-oriented finisher | Controls collision angle, keeps feet and drives through contact | Short yardage, loaded boxes, 1-on-1 safeties | Too much willingness to absorb direct hits |
| Explosive perimeter runner | Creates separation through acceleration and speed | Toss, pin-pull, wide zone, screens | Bounce instinct can turn useful interior gains into losses |
| Passing-down specialist | Protection ID, scanning, releases, receiving | Third down, two-minute, blitz-sensitive games | May offer less early-down power or between-tackles specialization |
| Multipurpose adaptable back | Efficient across tracks and situations | Multiple personnels and game plans | No singular overwhelming mismatch |

These are not mutually exclusive. A real back might combine one-cut discipline with excellent open-field contact avoidance, or downhill decisiveness with sophisticated duo patience. **Scheme fit is two-way:** coaches can modify alignments, mesh timing, and landmarks to a back's natural acceleration profile, while the player still needs discipline to execute the call. Elijah Brooks's coaching material specifically emphasizes alignment, landmark, read, steps, tempo, and adapting concepts to the backs available [S9].

### 23. Why a slower back can consistently gain more

Consider two backs with identical blocking. Back A has elite top speed but reaches the intended entry too fast, cuts late, and drifts into backside pursuit. Back B has modest top speed but presses the read correctly, plants before losing balance, and arrives just inside the blocker's favorable hip. Back B gains more even though his raw sprint ceiling is lower.

The explanation is **football-speed efficiency**: how much useful forward distance a player produces from a changing running picture. Contributing factors include recognition latency, correct selection of the cut, path efficiency, conservation of momentum, recovery-step cost, and contact avoidance. Pure open-field speed matters much more once the back is cleanly through the defense.

Separate **vision** from **decisiveness**: a runner can identify the best lane but be slow to commit; another can be decisive but wrong. Separate **patience** from **hesitation**: a patient runner is buying a specific blocking or defender reaction, while a hesitant runner is postponing a decision without acquiring useful new information. This distinction is indispensable in film evaluation and simulation ratings.

### 24. Fronts, run fits, and the extra defender

Run-game quality changes with defensive structure. Against a four-down Over front, the runner often gets a specific 3-technique and 1-technique relationship. Against Tite/Mint, the 4i surfaces can close interior escape routes. Against Bear, inside offensive linemen may be covered, reducing clean combo climbs. A slant can displace first-level defenders while a scraping LB fills the vacated area. The back needs to recognize **who is replacing the vacated gap**, not merely celebrate that the lineman moved.

The secondary matters. In single-high coverage, a safety may be available as a middle-field pursuit player while a corner or overhang supplies force depending on the call. In split-safety coverage, an alley safety may need to read the receiver's release before supporting the run, or may have immediate run-support responsibility under another check. These duties alter available arrival times at the perimeter. A runner should feel the net effect through the pursuit geometry, not assume that a two-high shell always means a light box.

**Unblocked does not mean unaccounted for.** On some QB-read concepts, the quarterback accounts for a defensive end. On an RPO, a receiver route or QB decision may influence a linebacker. Motion can change who fits the D gap. The RB still runs the correct track, but the value of his options depends on these other offensive threats.

### 25. Extraordinary plays and bad process: avoiding result-based coaching

A ten-yard gain can contain a bad RB read rescued by a missed tackle. A two-yard gain can represent excellent process when the OL suffers immediate backfield penetration. Grade the decision against the picture available **when the runner had to choose**, not against the hole that opened after he was already committed. Replay every snap at the first decisive plant, and ask whether an alternative path was physically reachable given the runner's velocity, balance, and field orientation.

An advanced grade can record: initial landmark, eye key, first-level interpretation, second-level prediction, decision timing, footwork cost, first-contact quality, and secure finish. Record unknowns honestly: broadcast angles often do not show the LB the runner was reading, and a camera cut can obscure initial penetration. Avoid claiming certainty from the final path alone.

## PART IV — Becoming a complete running back

### 26. Pass protection: assignment before collision

Running backs protect the passer with *information and timing*, not just toughness. In many systems, the QB/center sets a count point and the RB receives a scan, man, dual-read, or inside-out rule. Protection families differ; a “Mike” declaration is often an accounting point rather than an anatomical identification of a middle linebacker. The RB has to know what the offensive line is responsible for and which rusher will become his threat if more defenders enter the pressure.

The first priority is **identify the most dangerous assigned rush threat**. That may be an inside A-gap linebacker in a double-mug look, an outside corner coming off the slot, or a delayed green-dog rusher following the back's blocking action. A back who watches the edge exclusively can miss a direct interior threat; a back who shoots inside too quickly can expose an unblocked outside rusher. Successful scans are structured and protection-specific, not a universal left-to-right glance.

A plausible six-man half-slide example: the OL is sliding left and the RB scans from the backside inside linebacker toward the right edge according to the call. If the first threat drops and the second comes, he must redirect and protect the QB's actual launch point. In another protection family the assignments can be opposite. The engine should store declared responsibility and post-snap rule separately.

**Contact technique:** move toward the threat with an appropriate angle; break down under control; maintain a base rather than lunging; strike with usable hands and elbows inside; keep the head safely positioned out of direct helmet contact; and replace feet when absorbing the rusher. A smaller back may not stop a larger rusher dead, but he can redirect the rush path and delay the arrival long enough for the throw. Technique should account for edge-speed versus interior-power attacks.

A protection success grade must account for **time and path to QB**, not just whether the defender displaced the RB. Taking a rusher around the outside arc may be adequate even if the back yields ground. Being collapsed immediately into the QB's stepping lane is not. If no threat appears, check-release can turn the back into a receiver; he should not release prematurely when a delayed rusher remains his responsibility.

### 27. Receiving: route responsibilities, leverage, and transitions

A receiving back is not simply an RB with a catch rating. He needs to understand eligibility, release access, timing, coverage leverage, and the quarterback's progression. Common assignments include swing, shoot/arrow, checkdown, angle/Texas, option/choice, wheel, screen release, seam, and flat-to-wheel variations. A route may be designed as primary, a pressure outlet, a horizontal stretch, or a coverage indicator.

The **transition from protection to route** is particularly hard. A back must confirm the assigned pressure threat is absent or secure before leaving; then accelerate without drifting into a teammate's route window. A late release may still be valuable if the quarterback is extending the play, but an early route against an unaccounted blitz is a protection failure.

Versus man coverage, a back can manipulate a linebacker by selling an outside path before cutting inside on an angle route. Versus zone, he may need to throttle into open grass while retaining a throwing lane and avoiding a closing hook/curl defender. The RB should not be clairvoyant about the coverage: his decision depends on observed leverage and coached rules.

At the catch, transition matters more than a dramatic one-handed attempt. Track the ball, secure the catch without exposing the tip, immediately locate the nearest defender, protect the ball against first contact, and use the catch momentum to reach the best exit angle. A back catching a swing route with his shoulders already toward the sideline can be slower to turn upfield than one who aligns his torso and feet toward the anticipated outlet.

### 28. Screens: slow, sell, release, follow

A successful screen asks the RB to coordinate deception with blocking release timing. On a middle screen, he may initially display pass-protection posture, allow rushers to work upfield, then release behind the line to the planned catch point. On a slip screen, his first move may be a short collision or chip before slipping away. On an outside/quick screen, the receiver or RB may have the ball almost immediately and read force and leverage outside.

The runner's **first read after the catch** isn't the biggest open space on camera; it is the relationship among the first lead blocker, force defender, and pursuit angle. If the lead block secures outside leverage, the RB may cut behind it. If the defender defeats the block toward the inside, the runner may need to stretch farther or get vertical behind another blocker. Screens work partly because rush depth and line release rearrange pursuit, and those are time-sensitive interactions.

Blocking legality varies with whether the pass is forward or backward and with timing relative to the throw and line of scrimmage. Coaches and simulations must use the applicable rulebook; offensive ineligible-player-downfield, pass interference, holding, and illegal contact are not abstract penalties [S8]. Avoid treating early second-level OL contact on a forward screen as automatically legal.

### 29. Ball security: from mesh through violent contact

Ball security is a continuous technique: establish the receiving pocket, clamp the near point, secure the ball against the ribs, cover its end as the situation allows, and protect it through cuts and contact. Terminology such as **high and tight** or **five points of pressure** is common but implementations vary by staff. Exact hand position is less important than reliable contact, orientation, and transition timing under stress.

During a break, the RB should avoid loosening the ball to swing his arms freely. On the perimeter, switch arms only when useful and safe, with a controlled transfer rather than a dangerous exposed exchange. In traffic, cover up earlier when multiple defenders can rake the ball; after breaking through, regain a running position that preserves speed without leaving the ball unprotected.

A fumble mechanism should distinguish **poor initial handoff exchange**, **ball separation during a cut**, **defender punch/rake at contact**, **loss of grip during collision**, and **failed recovery while going to ground**. These have different coaching remedies. A generic ball-security rating can tune the probabilities, but should not replace observed physical causation.

### 30. Short yardage, goal line, and four-minute offense

In short yardage, the value of a cut is different: securing the first down may matter more than producing an explosive play. A back should understand required distance, defender penetration, offensive line surge, QB sneak alternatives, and the risk that an extra lateral move causes a loss. A downhill entry with compact steps and deliberate fall-forward control may be more useful than an elaborate jump cut.

At the goal line, defenders fit aggressively and field space is reduced. A back may be asked to attack a specific interior seam before the defensive line can create penetration, or to stretch the defense before a cut. The same scheme can require different tempo because the back has less depth before a defender contacts him and the payoff to a risky perimeter bounce may be different.

In **four-minute offense**, the runner needs to understand clock mechanics and ball security. Staying inbounds can have more strategic value than two extra yards out of bounds. In **two-minute offense**, pass protection, receiving, route alignment, and awareness of sideline/clock become central. A back's style should be situation-sensitive: a high-variance bounce on first-and-ten may be acceptable in some contexts but poor on third-and-one.

### 31. Conditioning, durability, and style under fatigue

Fatigue should not be a flat -5 rating to every RB skill. It can affect repeated acceleration, footwork precision, balance recovery, grip consistency, and mental speed differently. A runner who remains fast late in a game but loses plant stability may begin taking wider turns and inviting first contact. A physical back whose upright contact finishes become less stable may need to rely more on avoidance.

Contact exposure also matters. The engine can track accumulated hits, especially whether they occurred square through the torso or were glancing/rolling contacts, without pretending to diagnose injury from a contact vector. A tired back may become more conservative about extra cuts, or conversely become reckless because reaction quality degrades. Both are plausible player-specific tendencies requiring calibration.

In coaching practice, treat durability and load management separately from technique quality. There is no reason to simulate unsafe head-first collisions as an attribute of toughness. The best “finishing” mechanics should protect the player while preserving available yardage.

## PART V — Worked snap laboratories

> In each laboratory the stated offensive rules are **example installation rules**, not claims that every team using the same play name must coach identical aiming points or reads. The initial picture is deliberately incomplete. Film graders and simulation developers should preserve that uncertainty.

### Laboratory A — Inside zone right vs Over: the 3-tech crosses face

**Situation:** Shotgun RB offset left; inside zone right. Defense in an Over look, with a playside 3-technique and a backside shade; nickel is outside the tackle. **Coached track:** press the interior playside surface and key the playside defensive tackle's leverage through the guard's block.

**Snap:** the 3-tech spikes across the guard's inside shoulder. The guard's initial leverage is lost but his contact redirects the defender toward the A gap. The runner must distinguish a gap that is *visually open* from one the DL is about to occupy. A downhill jump into that gap gets stopped. The center has secured the backside shade long enough to create a tight adjacent seam, while a linebacker is flowing over the top.

**Good execution:** keep shoulders square at the mesh, maintain the interior press briefly, recognize the crossing face, use a compact plant behind the movement, and accelerate through the surviving crease. **Bad process despite possible gain:** prematurely bounce outside the nickel solely because the intended first gap was contaminated. **Film checkpoint:** where were the RB's eyes at the defender's first cross-face step, and could he physically reach the alternative before the LB filled?

### Laboratory B — Wide zone left: selling the edge before cutting inside

**Situation:** Under-center wide zone left; play-side tackle trying to reach a 5-tech; an overhang is force and a linebacker is stacked inside. **Coached track:** outside leg of the attached surface; commit vertically once the edge and overhang define the lane.

**Snap:** the RB presses the outside landmark. The overhang widens to keep force; the LB scrapes across the tackle's outside hip. The tackle cannot seal the 5-tech outward, but he can run him toward the sideline. A lane develops inside this flow.

**Good execution:** maintain lateral pressure until the overhang has committed, plant once, get upfield on the inside hip of the tackle's wash, secure against backside pursuit. **Common error:** the RB turns vertical immediately because the initial C gap looks open, allowing the LB to meet him square before the reach block develops. **Counterfactual:** hold every defender's abilities constant; move the RB's vertical cut two steps earlier and see whether the LB's interception probability rises. The point of the drill is *tempo creating the gap*.

### Laboratory C — Duo: point linebacker fills versus scrapes

**Situation:** Duo right, attached tight end, two interior doubles. A coaching point identifies the Mike as the primary read. **Branch one:** Mike flows quickly outside the play-side double. Pressing behind the double then inserting inside prevents the Mike from regaining inside leverage. **Branch two:** Mike plugs the interior crease. The RB may use the outside shoulder of the double or a defined backside option, but only if the edge and minus-one defender are controlled.

**Crucial variable:** the RB should not decide from the Mike's starting location alone; the **direction and timing of the fit relative to the double-team displacement** matters. **Bad process:** the back sees the Mike plug, instantly bounces four gaps without checking the force defender. **Film checkpoint:** did the RB pressure the linebacker to declare, or run sideways before the double teams earned their leverage?

### Laboratory D — GT Counter against a wrong-arm edge

**Situation:** Counter right following jet motion left. First puller is responsible for the edge; second puller wraps for LB. **Snap:** the edge attacks the first puller with an inside shoulder, attempting to spill the ball outside. The first puller cannot execute a normal kick-out; he makes contact and redirects the edge inward. The wrap must adjust to the new lane location.

**Good execution:** the runner keys the edge/first-puller relationship, does not force the original inside path against a spilled block, and uses the installed response—log outside or redirect behind the second puller—while accounting for force. **Common error:** the RB stares at the second puller and runs into the first collision. **Simulation criterion:** the alternative gap must emerge from real block displacement; never add a “counter-wrong-arm +3 yards” bonus.

### Laboratory E — Split zone versus a crashing end and scrape exchange

**Situation:** Split zone left from pistol. H-back slices across toward a backside edge. The backside edge crashes inward as a backside LB scrapes around for contain. **Snap:** the H-back contact is late because the edge has squeezed rapidly. The RB sees a tempting cutback lane just inside the disappearing end. The scraping LB is entering the lane from behind at speed.

**Good execution:** preserve the intended interior track and weigh the backside possibility against the scrape's arrival time. A cutback may be bad even if the DL's movement appears to open a gap. **Advanced defensive takeaway:** the defender who vacates the gap can be deliberately replaced by another player. **Film checkpoint:** whether the RB's peripheral vision tracked the scrape as he considered bending behind the slice.

### Laboratory F — Power in a tight box: press the insert blocker's hip

**Situation:** Power right on third-and-two; a fullback kicks the edge and the backside guard pulls to the playside linebacker. **Snap:** the fullback secures an outside seal while the linebacker takes two downhill steps toward the intended B-gap entry. The pulling guard contacts the linebacker's inside shoulder and rotates him outward.

**Good execution:** RB presses the gap to hold the LB, enters tight off the puller's favorable hip, and gets forward with compact steps. **Bad process:** the RB cuts outside the puller despite adequate inside leverage, losing the first-down angle. **Bad mechanics:** shoulders open unnecessarily, forcing a second cut when forward momentum matters most. **Outcome metric:** first-down probability from the point of commitment, not just raw yards.

### Laboratory G — Trap: penetration creates the lane

**Situation:** Inside trap left. A 3-tech penetrates vertically when the guard invites him upfield. The trapper crosses behind the line to strike. **Good execution:** the RB maintains the trap track despite the alarming penetration, identifies that the 3-tech is passing the aiming point, receives the handoff securely, and accelerates behind the trap block. **Failure branch:** the 3-tech recognizes trap, squeezes, and constricts the space before the trapper arrives; now the RB must use another coached outlet rather than follow a ghost lane.

**Film checkpoint:** a runner who immediately bounces away from the penetrating defender may have destroyed the very leverage that the play intended to create. The reason to stay patient is not blind trust; it is a prediction about the trapper's angle and arrival.

### Laboratory H — Draw into a pass-rush lane

**Situation:** Second-and-eight; offense shows pass, defense rushes four with wide interior penetration. The back first assumes pass-pro-like posture then receives the delayed handoff. **Snap:** the right defensive tackle rushes outside the guard and the LB drops at pass depth. The center can turn upfield at the proper moment. **Good execution:** the back waits long enough to draw the defense, receives the ball under control, then attacks the vacated interior lane before the LB diagnoses the draw. **Failure:** the back starts downhill as soon as the ball is snapped, alerting the linebacker and destroying the deception. **Film checkpoint:** how much did rush depth and LB movement change *before* the draw was declared?

### Laboratory I — Pass protection versus double-A mug

**Situation:** The offense calls a six-man protection with RB scanning from the defined inside threat toward the backside edge. Both LBs initially mug the A gaps. **Snap:** the first linebacker drops into a low hole while the second delays then attacks the QB's near-side interior lane. **Good execution:** the RB does not launch at the dropping defender, redirects to the live rusher, establishes a stable base, and contests the rush path long enough for the throw. **Failure:** the RB picks a pre-snap jersey and hits air while the delayed rusher comes free. **Film checkpoint:** did he read the actual pressure or merely react to mug presentation?

### Laboratory J — Swing reception versus outside-in force

**Situation:** RB releases on a swing to the right. A corner/nickel is the outside-in force player while a linebacker is pursuing inside-out. **Catch:** the RB secures the pass moving laterally; the force player widens and the LB closes from the inside. **Good execution:** look up after securing the ball, assess the two tackle angles, get vertical behind the first perimeter block if it holds, or use the defender's overrun to plant inside. **Bad process:** sprint toward the boundary because it looks open, allowing the force player to compress the space. **Film checkpoint:** compare the actual gain with the path available at ball arrival, not with the open space two seconds later.

### Laboratory K — Four-minute offense: choosing the less spectacular finish

**Situation:** Leading late, first-and-ten with the clock running. Zone right produces a four-yard crease, with a tempting sideline bounce that might gain twelve but risks an out-of-bounds finish or negative play. The defense has sufficient contain and pursuit to make that bounce uncertain.

**Good execution:** attack the secure interior lane, protect the ball through first contact, and stay inbounds. **Counterfactual:** on the same blocking picture while trailing by a touchdown late, the risk-reward calculation can favor trying for the explosive outside path. **Mechanics do not change; tactical utility does.** This distinction belongs in the RB decision system.

## PART VI — How to coach, evaluate, and simulate the position

### 32. A developmental progression from feet to full 11-on-11

A valuable practice progression begins **without defenders**, then introduces one read defender, then a changing second-level picture, then coordinated OL/DL. The goal isn't to teach rote foot patterns divorced from football. Each drill should use the actual play's alignment, landmark, and tempo so the player trains the same perceptual and motor problem that arises in a game [S1, S2, S9].

| Drill | Setup | Coaching assessment |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Track/mesh | QB and markers, correct back alignment | Entry angle, secure mesh, first three steps |
| Press-and-plant | Two cones as OL heels, read DL moves | Square body, correct plant, minimal wasted steps |
| Double-team vision | Two OL, one DL, one LB | Did RB respond to defender fit or arbitrary daylight? |
| Stretch-to-vertical | Reach-block representation, force player | Preserved edge stress then cut north-south |
| Puller timing | Kick-out/insert dummy or moving OL | Did runner arrive beside the correct hip? |
| Wind-back | Changing backside gap, late scrape LB | One-foot cut when appropriate; recognizes replacement |
| Controlled contact | Pads, variable angle, safe technique | Glancing contact, base recovery, ball protection |
| Pass-pro scan | Mug/drop/late blitz simulation | Assignment identification and stable collision angle |
| Swing catch/turn | Moving catch then angle defender | Secured catch before cut, identified leverage |
| Full inside run | OL/DL/LB with actual front/stunt calls | Read, tempo, path, and finishing across all levels |

A drill can be deceptive if its answer is always preplanned. If the defender always moves left and the runner always cuts right, the back may learn choreography rather than recognition. Randomize the legal defender responses *within the coaching picture* and require the runner to explain what he saw. Include reps where the best decision is to take three yards without a dramatic move.

### 33. A film-grading worksheet that separates process from outcome

Grade each snap in four stages. **Before snap:** was the RB aligned correctly and aware of the likely pressure/force picture? **At mesh:** was footwork and ball security correct? **At decision:** did the runner press the right landmark, read the intended player/block, and choose a physically reachable lane? **After entry:** did he manage first contact, pursue efficient yardage, and obey the situation?

A suggested scale for each stage is -2 (major error), -1 (minor deficiency), 0 (acceptable), +1 (very good), +2 (exceptional). The sum is a coaching tool, not a scientifically validated RB metric. Attach a confidence field: “clear,” “probable,” or “unknown,” especially when film does not show the RB's eyes or intended landmark. Do not give a +2 for a long run unless the decision really was sound.

Record a **wrong read rescued by talent** separately from a **correct read defeated by superior defense**. A back's strengths and weaknesses become legible after many snaps with a known scheme: early bounce frequency, false-cut rate, post-cut acceleration, missed backside recognition, pass-pro assignment errors, and dangerous ball-handling events.

### 34. Quantifying lane quality without giving the back omniscience

A simulation can calculate the objective field state at every time step while letting the RB perceive only part of it. The internal validator can estimate lane value using entry width, path distance, probability of early collision, time before closure, the next defender's interception angle, down/distance, and the runner's locomotion state.

One illustrative model is:

`Value(lane) = expected forward progress − collision/turnover risk − lost-time cost + situation-specific reward`.

This is a modeling heuristic, not a law of football. At first, implement it as an interpretable score so developers can see whether the runner chose a poor lane because of bad visual information, bad evaluation, or a movement failure. Later, calibrate its factors from tracking trajectories and human film labels.

NFL Next Gen Stats' **expected rushing yards** framework considers spatial relationships among all players at the handoff; **rushing yards over expected** compares actual gains with that expectation [S10]. This is valuable for aggregate evaluation, but it does not tell you exactly what a runner saw or whether one specific first plant was coached correctly. Do not train your policy to optimize only RYOE; it can hide scheme discipline, play-clock constraints, and selection effects.

### 35. A decision engine for a believable running back

I would implement a **partially observed hierarchical controller**:

1. **Call rules:** Store intended track, blocker read, linebacker read, default bang/bend/bounce outlets, mesh timing, and forbidden premature departures.
2. **Belief state:** Infer whether a controlling block is winning, defender's fit direction, likelihood of scrape replacement, and edge integrity from visible cues; include uncertainty.
3. **Action candidates:** Continue press, modulate tempo, make a one-foot cut, jump cut, bounce, bend, accelerate, stiff-arm, cover ball, or terminate pursuit with an inbounds finish.
4. **Mechanical feasibility:** Reject cuts the RB cannot physically execute from current velocity, planted foot, orientation, balance, and contact state.
5. **Short-horizon planning:** Estimate the near-future consequences of the remaining actions, with situational utility and a calibrated error/variation term.
6. **Execution:** Issue footwork, stride, torso, hand, and acceleration controls; let movement/contact physics determine what actually occurs.
7. **Feedback:** Update belief after blocks, defender steps, collisions, and teammate motion; continue through tackle or whistle.

A good runner's edge should mainly emerge from **earlier recognition, better predictions, better-timed commitment, efficient planting, and favorable collision management**—not a hidden trait that increases yards whenever the player touches the ball.

**Cognitive limitations are essential.** The runner cannot know a hidden defensive assignment before the defender reveals it. A defense can deceive his initial read through slant/scrape, backer blitz rotation, or overhang replacement. A talented RB can still be wrong. Similarly, the RB shouldn't see precisely which gap will open a full second in the future; he can forecast a likely block outcome based on observable leverage.

### 36. Movement and contact variables worth actually simulating

The 2D motion + vertical/contact abstraction is enough if a player's physical state is richer than position/speed. Track position, velocity, heading, shoulder/hip facing, stance width, planted foot or load phase, step timing, center-of-mass offset, upper-body lean, balance, ball arm, free-hand readiness, visual target, and current opponent contact. For every block, track who controls which shoulder/hip, contact direction, force duration, and whether the defender is being displaced or rotated.

The RB needs a model of **time to entry** and **time to next viable plant**. A highly agile player at full speed cannot take a 90-degree cut without a braking/plant cost. A power runner making square contact from a stable base should not be displaced like the same runner caught mid-jump. A defender arriving at a lane two frames after the RB leaves should not retroactively invalidate the cut.

Separate locomotion physics from named football moves. “Jump cut” should request a specific sequence of takeoff, lateral displacement, landing, and reacceleration; it cannot produce an unphysical instant teleport. “Get skinny” should modify shoulder profile and contact exposure, not delete the RB's collision box. “Stiff arm” should create contact through the free hand envelope with timing, geometry, and opponent counteractions.

### 37. Player styles as coherent parameter profiles

Give each RB a compact parameter profile rather than unrelated hard-coded run results. Physical parameters: straight acceleration, lateral braking capacity, stride-adjustment speed, collision mass, grip endurance, effective upper-body strength, and balance-recovery time. Cognitive parameters: read recognition latency, linebacker-anticipation accuracy, false-positive rate for cutback openings, patience threshold, adaptability, and defensive-rotation recognition. Technique parameters: efficient plant cost, shoulder narrowing, hand/ball control, pass-pro strike timing, catch transition, and stalk-defense evasion.

Then assign **preferences**: early downhill commitment, willingness to bounce, risk tolerance by down and distance, preference for one-foot plants versus jump cuts, frequency of aggressive shoulder finishes, and ability to delay for blockers. Preferences are not immutable: the runner learns and conditions actions on scheme, game state, defensive tendencies, and coaching instructions.

A “patient back” should not magically slow everyone else down. He should make different choices about when to press and plant, within the same physical world. A “one-cut back” should be efficient at conserving speed through the correct turn. A “power back” should be better at arranging and surviving certain contact states. These are mechanistically meaningful distinctions.

### 38. Acceptance tests before adding more running styles

- **Track discipline:** against the same front, a back preserves the scheme's track unless the coached read invalidates it.
- **One-foot vs jump cut:** for a moderate angle change, the efficient planted cut generally reaches useful vertical speed faster; the jump cut becomes useful for sufficiently large lateral movement or sudden obstacle avoidance.
- **Press effect:** delaying the vertical cut on wide zone can move a linebacker laterally and change the eventual lane, but waiting too long lets pursuit recover.
- **LB manipulation:** on duo, a back can alter the LB's commitment by pressing a double-team landmark, without controlling the LB's mind.
- **Wrong-arm counter:** a spilled kick block changes the actual open space; the RB adapts if the correct information arrives in time.
- **Ball security under contact:** fumbles respond coherently to exposed ball orientation and targeted defender contact, not merely random possession flips.
- **Pass-pro cognition:** a RB can sort drop/simulated rush versus live pressure; late recognition carries measurable time cost.
- **Styles matter:** two backs with equal straight-line speed but different plant efficiency/recognition produce meaningfully different run distributions.
- **Scheme fit matters:** changing only the offensive concept alters the relative advantages of patient, one-cut, and contact-oriented backs.
- **Counterfactual reproducibility:** fixed random seeds and state logs let a developer determine why a snap changed when one skill or alignment changed.

## A condensed coaching checklist

**Before the snap:** know the play family, initial track, aiming point, first-level key, second-level key, unblocked defenders, mesh, and the game situation. **At the mesh:** get the ball on time, preserve posture and vision, and arrive at the landmark at the prescribed tempo. **At the read:** pressure the correct defender/block, interpret leverage, choose a reachable opening, plant efficiently, and immediately regain vertical acceleration. **After entry:** set up the next defender, protect the ball, minimize clean contact, maintain body control, and finish in a way that serves down, distance, and clock.

## Glossary of important terms

**Aiming point:** initial coached landmark to attack, distinct from final entry. **Bang:** downhill use of a viable immediate crease. **Bend:** redirect behind front or linebacker flow. **Bounce:** take an exterior route when edge leverage supports it. **Box:** defender technique keeping a run inside. **Crack of center:** center-related aiming landmark used in some inside-zone installations. **Cutback:** change toward backside/behind defensive flow; may or may not require a jump cut. **Decision point:** moment the available football and physical options are evaluated. **Duo:** no-puller vertical double-team scheme often keyed to an LB fit. **Force:** defender responsible for keeping an exterior run from escaping, per defensive rules. **Gap:** offensive line interval used as a football reference. **Get skinny:** mechanically reduce exposure through a crease without losing useful momentum. **Green dog:** defender who blitzes when a man he is covering stays in to protect. **Kick-out:** block aiming to move an edge defender outward. **Lag:** nose or interior DL technique in which the defender works across the blocker toward the opposite gap. **Landmark:** intended path reference (lineman leg, hip, or other coaching target). **Mesh:** QB/RB handoff or exchange phase. **Minus-one:** particular defensive fitter/read reference used by some offensive teaching systems. **Overhang:** outside linebacker/nickel at the box-perimeter interface. **Press:** maintain a threat to a landmark to influence defenders/blockers. **RPO:** run-pass option in which QB chooses based on a defined read. **Scrape exchange:** defensive movement in which a LB replaces a gap/edge vacated by a moving DL. **Spill:** redirect the ball to another defender by attacking the inside portion of a block. **Track:** RB's intended path to and through the read. **Trap:** blocking concept using an invite-and-strike angle on a penetrating defender. **Verticalize:** reorient from lateral/waiting track toward forward running. **Wind back:** coaching term used in some systems for a quick backside redirection with efficient footwork. **Wrong-arm:** edge technique attacking the inside shoulder of a kick-out to spill the run.
