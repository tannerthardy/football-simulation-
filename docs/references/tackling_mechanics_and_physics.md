## 1  What a tackle actually is

Tackling is best understood as a series of linked physical tasks. The defender must find a reachable path to the runner, arrive with controllable body position, establish useful contact, and generate enough combined collision and ground reaction to redirect, slow, unbalance, or contain the runner. A grip is helpful but not sufficient by itself. [1,3]

> **Diagram description — five phases:** Track (observe and align) → Prepare (brake and position) → Connect (first contact) → Drive (ground force) → Finish (control through the end of the play).

*USA Football describes five coaching “fights”: track, prepare, connect, accelerate, and finish. These are a teaching framework, not five independent laws of physics. [1]*

### What the defender must solve before contact

- **Space and leverage.** Close the runner’s available lane without overrunning the near hip. Containing the sideline, cutback, or inside escape may matter more than taking the shortest path.
- **Approach speed.** A fast arrival can create a large collision impulse, but high speed also raises braking demand and makes a late cut more difficult to accommodate.
- **Body preparation.** The feet, hips, trunk and head must arrive in a useful orientation. A defender running at full speed may not have time to develop a planted base.
- **Post-contact control.** The runner may bounce, twist, keep driving, lose a step, or stumble. Both athletes continue reacting while contact forces change.

### Three different defensive wins

| Stop | Redirect | Contain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The runner’s forward progress is arrested or reversed. | The runner’s path changes, even if they keep moving. | The runner is kept inbounds or forced to a boundary/teammate, whether or not a fall occurs. |

> **Key principle:** A tackle can succeed without a dramatic hit: a defender may erase the escape angle, constrain forward progress, or keep a runner in a position where assistance arrives.

## 2  Pursuit angles, deceleration and preparation

The path to contact determines the defender’s options at impact. The relevant quantity is relative motion: the position and velocity of the runner compared with the defender, not the speed of either athlete in isolation.

> **Diagram description — three approaches:** A head-on defender and runner move toward one another; an oblique defender approaches across the runner’s path; a trailing defender chases from behind. The direction and relative speed of approach change the possible contact and braking response.

### Closing speed and interception

A defender pursuing a moving runner must account for where the runner is likely to be when they arrive. Pursuing the current location tends to create trailing contact; overcommitting to the predicted location can create an overrun when the runner cuts. Anticipation, perceptual delay, speed and acceleration all affect the available geometry.

**Relative velocity:** v<sub>rel</sub> = v<sub>runner</sub> − v<sub>defender</sub>. A large relative approach speed increases the rapidity of contact, but does not alone determine the final tackle.

### Why late braking is difficult

For idealized constant braking acceleration, the required stopping distance is **d = v² / (2a)**. Doubling entry speed roughly quadruples the distance required to stop if the achievable braking acceleration is unchanged.

**Illustration:** At 6 m/s with 6 m/s² constant braking, stopping distance is 3 m. At 8 m/s under the same braking, it is 5.33 m. These are calculation examples, not measured universal football braking capacities. Real plants are directional, time-varying and constrained by turf, body orientation and muscular force.

### Why the last few steps matter

A shorter controlled step can position the base underneath the torso for a direction change or collision. Overstriding may place the foot too far ahead, limiting the ability to adjust. In one youth-football training study, larger tackle steps were associated with higher odds of head acceleration threshold exceedance; the result does not establish an optimal step length for NFL athletes. [5]

> **Key principle:** A clean tackle often begins before the shoulder arrives: the defender must arrive with enough remaining traction, foot placement and visual control to react to the runner’s last movement.

## 3  Momentum, impulse and the initial hit

The most useful starting laws are momentum and impulse. Translational momentum is **p = mv**; net external impulse changes total momentum: **J<sub>ext</sub> = Δp = ∫F<sub>ext</sub>dt**. For a tackler–runner pair, contact forces are internal to the pair, but turf reactions are external.

> **Diagram description — opposing momenta:** A 90 kg defender traveling at 3 m/s toward a 100 kg runner traveling in the opposite direction at 4 m/s. Arrows depict their initial motion; numbers are an illustrative example.

*Two opposing athletes before contact. Body positions and arrows are schematic; the numbers below are an idealized calculation, not measured football data.*

### Worked example: what a collision can and cannot do

Suppose a 100 kg runner moves at +4 m/s and a 90 kg defender approaches at −3 m/s. If they were to stick together briefly with negligible external impulse during the collision, conservation of linear momentum gives:

**v<sub>after</sub> = (100 × 4 + 90 × −3) / (100 + 90) ≈ +0.68 m/s**

Their combined velocity would still be in the runner’s original direction. If the collision were perfectly inelastic, the relative translational kinetic energy lost would be about 1.16 kJ. In reality, football players neither form rigid blocks nor stick permanently together: the interaction includes deformations, slipping, rotation, stepping, arm force and continued ground loading.

### Force depends on time as well as momentum change

Average net force obeys **F<sub>avg</sub> = Δp / Δt**. A short impulse can generate a high average force; prolonging deceleration can reduce average force for the same momentum change. But peak contact force depends on compliance, effective mass, posture and the time profile of force—not just this average.

In a rugby tackle-bag experiment, peak forces differed between stationary dominant-shoulder (2.84 ± 0.74 kN), stationary nondominant-shoulder (2.44 ± 0.64 kN), and moving dominant-shoulder conditions (3.40 ± 0.86 kN). These are controlled bag-test values, not NFL collision norms. [6]

## 4  Ground reaction, stance and balance

One of the largest misconceptions is that a tackle is determined by the two athletes’ collision momentum alone. While players have cleats on turf, the ground can exert a substantial external impulse through their feet. That extra momentum exchange is why a supported defender can resist or redirect a heavier runner.

> **Diagram description — free-body forces:** A defender receives the runner’s contact force while gravity acts downward, the ground provides an upward normal reaction, and cleat–turf friction can provide horizontal reaction. These forces evolve during the tackle.

*Simplified force diagram of a defender interacting with a moving runner. The actual directions of ground force and contact load change throughout a tackle.*

### The friction limit is a constraint, not a promise

A simple dry-friction approximation is **|F<sub>horizontal</sub>| ≤ μN**, where N is normal loading and μ an effective friction coefficient. It is useful conceptually, but cleat–turf interaction can depend on shoe design, penetration, shear direction, surface moisture, and loading history.

**Example:** An assumed effective coefficient μ = 0.8 for a 100 kg athlete under 981 N normal load implies an approximate horizontal force bound of 785 N in this simplified model. The number is illustrative and should not be interpreted as measured turf traction or a fixed per-athlete capacity.

### Base of support and body position

- **Center of mass (COM).** The location of the body’s mass relative to the planted base changes the ease of recovering a perturbation.
- **Braking versus driving.** Feet may need to slow the defender before contact, resist motion during contact, then reaccelerate to maintain control.
- **Stance width and direction.** A wider base can help resist some perturbations, but a wide or crossed stance can also make the next step slower. The useful base depends on force direction.
- **Failed support.** Once a defender is airborne or sliding, the ability to generate a new horizontal ground reaction is sharply reduced, though existing momentum and a grip still matter.

> **Key principle:** Strength is not the same as usable force. To influence the runner, muscle effort must be transferred through a viable path: ground → legs → trunk → shoulder/arms → carrier.

## 5  Off-center hits: leverage, torque and rotation

Football contacts rarely pass directly through both players’ centers of mass. Even a perfectly timed tackle can cause both bodies to rotate. This is not wasted or mysterious force: it follows from where the impulse is applied.

> **Diagram description — off-center contact:** The contact impulse acts at a position offset from the runner’s center of mass; the moment arm generates rotation in addition to linear momentum change.

*Top-down schematic: the same net contact impulse changes both linear momentum and angular momentum if its line of action misses the center of mass.*

**Rotational impulse:** **ΔL = r × J**. In two dimensions, the magnitude is **rJ sin(θ)**. The perpendicular moment arm—not merely the length of the arm—determines the angular impulse.

**Illustration:** A 200 N·s impulse acting with a 0.25 m perpendicular moment arm produces 50 N·m·s of angular impulse about that COM. This is a scale example, not a measured tackle. The resulting rotation depends on moment of inertia and opposing external moments.

### Why a shoulder, hip and thigh contact differ

A shoulder-level impact can strongly redirect an athlete’s trunk, while a lower-body contact can impede the runner’s stepping and produce tipping or a roll. Both can cause a fall, but through different combinations of translation, rotation and support loss. Lower contact does not automatically mean safer contact: head injury risk to tackler and carrier can differ by contact height. [7,8]

### The importance of controlling the line of force

A defender whose shoulder drives through the near hip with feet underneath may continue transferring ground-generated impulse after first contact. A defender whose arms reach across a moving body may create substantial rotation in themselves instead. Neither is predetermined: geometry, speed and post-contact steps set the result.

> **Key principle:** A useful mental model: contact point controls rotational leverage; the direction and duration of transmitted impulse control how strongly the runner’s velocity changes.

## 6  Wrapping, slipping and being dragged

A wrap changes the contest from a brief collision to a potentially sustained mechanical link. Arms can constrain relative translation, pull the athletes closer, or transmit a twisting load. However, a grip that stays attached can transmit little force when both athletes travel at nearly the same speed.

> **Diagram description — attached vs resisting:** In co-motion, a tackler and runner travel at similar velocities with little relative grip load. With a feasible planted stance, the defender can produce opposing load, provided the grip and support can sustain it.

*Two limiting cases. Left: the tackler and runner move together, so little relative grip stretch develops. Right: the defender attempts to resist, developing greater tension if their stance and grip can sustain it.*

### The difference between attachment and control

- **Momentary reach:** fingertips or one hand momentarily touch the runner but cannot necessarily sustain a large separating load.
- **Secure wrap:** the defender has improved purchase, but their arms, torso, and stance must still carry the transmitted forces.
- **Clamping the lower body:** sufficiently secure restraint may interrupt a step or alter leg motion; a glancing ankle touch may merely disturb stride.
- **Slipping and regripping:** a changing contact surface or rotational mismatch can progressively reduce security before complete separation.

### Three ways a held runner can continue moving

**1.** The defender has insufficient opposing ground reaction. **2.** The contact geometry produces rotation or motion along a direction the grip resists poorly. **3.** The carrier continues developing leg drive while the defender has no effective finishing leverage.

Sustained contact may also end because of a second defender, a boundary, forward progress being stopped, or the runner losing balance. A runner dragged forward several steps is not inconsistent with physics; the question is which forces the two bodies can sustain and how their balance changes.

## 7  The tackle types and expected mechanical outcomes

Technique labels describe typical coaching intent; actual events may blend types or change after contact. USA Football explicitly teaches form, thigh-and-drive, and thigh-and-roll as distinct starting frameworks. [1,13]

| Situation | Mechanical signature | Likely outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front-on form tackle | High relative closing speed, shoulder/torso contact, possible planted drive. | Immediate stop, short carry, rebound, fall, or failure to wrap. |
| 45° crossing tackle | Off-center impulse plus substantial yaw; foot recovery is difficult. | Redirection, co-rotation, roll down, or spin-off. |
| Side-on low wrap | Short impact lever, potential step interruption and rolling moment. | Trips or delayed fall; slipping arm/leg contact can fail. |
| Trailing ankle reach | Small opposing collision impulse, weak grip purchase, limited stance options. | Stumble after several steps, drag, recovery, or complete escape. |
| Thigh-and-drive | Low connected position plus continued ground impulse. | Progress halted or reversed if support and grip endure. |
| Thigh-and-roll | Clamped body/leg contact with coordinated rotation. | Controlled descent or broken connection if contact is lost. |
| High smother / wrap | Upper-body restraint; legs may retain driving room. | Containment, slowing, shared fall, or continued churn. |
| One-arm tackle | Small or short-lived contact patch with imperfect body control. | Glancing deviation or frequent slip, depending on context. |

### What not to infer from the label alone

A low tackle is not guaranteed to disrupt the runner’s legs; a high tackle is not guaranteed to leave them mobile. A “drive” technique can still fail if the defender is moving backward. Likewise, the most violent visible collision is not necessarily the most efficient defensive result.

Evidence caution: a 2025 rugby analysis of 52,204 tackles found active shoulder tackles generally associated with the highest probability of stopping progress and arm tackles with the lowest. Rates varied by playing level, contact height and tackle type; they are not NFL rates and observational differences should not be read as purely causal. [9]

## 8  The ball carrier is an active mechanical system

The runner is not passive during a tackle. Their ability to brace, plant, turn the hips, stiff-arm, lower the trunk, or continue stepping changes the relative velocity and contact position. Unlike two rigid objects, both athletes generate mechanical work throughout the encounter.

### Why a runner may break through

- **Momentum advantage:** a high-mass, high-speed carrier has substantial forward momentum, which requires an opposing net impulse to arrest.
- **Support and propulsion:** if the runner can keep useful feet underneath the COM, they can continue generating forward impulse through the turf.
- **Rotational escape:** a turn can reduce the effective restraint or move a contact toward the end of a lever.
- **Last-second cut:** the defender must brake and redirect; early commitment can carry them out of the runner’s path.
- **Contact sequencing:** initial shoulder impact might slow a runner, but a subsequent lost grip can still produce an escape.

### Yards after contact are not one physical event

Additional gain after first contact can arise from a broken wrap, a dragging tackler, a delayed fall, defensive assistance arriving late, a glancing hit, or a runner who briefly regained balance. These mechanisms should not be treated as interchangeable in film analysis. Tracking-based studies argue for measuring changes in forward velocity across proximity/contact windows rather than crediting only the final tackler. [11]

### Energy and work

A runner’s translational kinetic energy is **K = ½mv²**. A collision can convert some of it to deformation, internal motion and rotation. But muscle work and ground reactions can add further mechanical energy after the collision. Neither a simple energy-loss fraction nor a conservation-of-two-bodies calculation predicts the complete finishing phase.

> **Key principle:** Stopping the runner requires removing, redirecting, or preventing the regeneration of forward momentum—not merely achieving contact.

## 9  Gang tackles, assistance and forward progress

> **Diagram description — two defenders:** An initial tackler and a second defender apply loads in different directions to a runner. Their combined effect depends on each force vector, their moments, and each defender’s momentum and footing.

When another defender arrives, the relative geometry changes. One player may supply the initial slowdown, another a lateral redirection, and another may constrain the runner’s next step. The effective result depends on arrival timing, contact direction, how force passes through each defender, and the runner’s response.

### Why two defenders are not just twice one defender

Their impulses do not necessarily add in the same direction. Opposing lateral forces can reduce yaw while increasing restraint; simultaneous poorly aligned contact can also destabilize defenders. If both defenders lose balance, a runner may still carry the pile forward. Each player has their own ground support and momentum.

### The end of a play is not always a mechanical fall

An upright runner may be stopped by forward progress. A runner can also be carried backward while the ball remains dead at the forward-progress spot under the applicable rules. The visible physical state and the officiating decision are different descriptions of the play. [10]

### How to read a gang tackle on film

| First contact | Assistance | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did velocity slow or turn? Was there a true wrap? | When did help arrive? From which direction? | Did the runner fall, stop, reach a boundary, or escape? |
| Was the first defender planted or being carried? | Did the helper take the inside hip, outside hip, or lower body? | Which action plausibly changed momentum or balance? |

> **Key principle:** The final player credited with the tackle may not be the athlete who delivered the largest useful deceleration.

## 10  Injury mechanisms and legal boundaries

The same contact that changes the ball carrier’s motion can produce large head, neck, limb and joint loads. Mechanical effectiveness and injury risk overlap but are not identical objectives. Research does not establish a universally safest or most successful tackle for every contact geometry.

### Head and neck loading

Contact to the head/neck, direct helmet impacts and inertial head motion are distinct risks. Head acceleration can occur without direct head contact. A youth American football study measured 149 head-acceleration events across 125 tackles and associated higher graded tackling form with lower head kinematics on average; sample size and level limit generalization. [4]

An observational study of youth/middle/high-school football supported the head-up inside-shoulder technique and highlighted hazards of placing the head across the runner’s body. The study describes success associations; it is not proof of zero injury risk. [2]

### Contact height is a trade-off

Controlled rugby experiments and a systematic review show that tackle height and trunk posture affect head kinematics, and tackler and ball-carrier risks can move in opposite directions. One 455-trial experiment found lower-torso contact produced higher measured head acceleration for tacklers than mid/upper-torso conditions, despite different effects on carriers. This should not be translated directly into an NFL “safe zone.” [7,8]

### 2026 NFL rules and prohibited techniques

The current NFL rulebook prohibits the specified hip-drop technique (Rule 12-2-18), horse-collar tackles, impermissible use of the helmet and prohibited contact against defenseless players. A legal rolling finish must not be conflated with a prohibited hip-drop that traps the carrier’s lower legs. Consult the specific league’s rules; officiating definitions matter. [10]

| Risk factor | Why it matters mechanically |
| --- | --- |
| Head first / head across | Can expose the head and cervical spine to harmful direct or axial loads. |
| High closing speed | Increases available relative kinetic energy and makes contact preparation harder. |
| Poor feet / trunk position | Can reduce post-contact control, alter head position and increase collapse risk. |
| Leg trapping / prohibited drop | Can concentrate body-weight and twisting loads on lower limbs. |

## 11  What the evidence actually supports

The physics laws are general, but measured collision forces and injury relationships depend strongly on the study design. The most defensible conclusions combine controlled biomechanics, football video, coaching evidence, and league rules rather than treating one set of laboratory force values as universal.

| Evidence | What it found | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 832 high-school football tackles [3] | Higher graded finishing technique related to lower YAC; overall ratings predicted head-contact category. | Observational video scoring; not causal force measurement. |
| 149 youth head-acceleration events [4] | Better form scores associated with lower head kinematics on average. | Fourteen youth players; not NFL adults. |
| 15 rugby players, bag test [6] | Moving entries produced higher mean peak force than stationary entries. | Instrumented bag, not opponent interaction. |
| 455 controlled rugby tackles [8] | Tackle height and engagement altered tackler/carrier head acceleration differently. | Successful front-on torso tackles only. |
| 52,204 rugby tackles [9] | Arm tackles showed lower stop-success than active shoulder tackles across levels. | Different sport, observational probabilities. |
| Tracking-based tackling work [11] | Contributions can be measured from changes in forward motion across contact windows. | Tracking infers proximity, not actual grip forces. |

### What still cannot be confidently quantified

- A universal “typical” NFL grip force, tackle impulse, or ground reaction for each tackle type.
- The exact causal improvement in safety from any individual coaching cue across all ages and competition levels.
- True individual foot loads and arm torques from league-wide optical tracking alone.
- A fixed threshold of collision speed or moment arm that guarantees either a fall or an escape.

> **Key principle:** Physics gives the governing constraints. Film and biomechanics reveal how people use those constraints. Neither alone is a complete tackle explanation.

## 12  A field-film checklist for studying any tackle

When watching real film, separate the event into time-ordered observations rather than labeling the entire rep “good form” or “broken tackle.” Slowed or multi-angle footage makes the following questions more answerable.

| Stage | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Before contact | Where is the runner’s COM traveling? How much room and which escape lanes remain? What is the defender’s closing angle? |
| Final two steps | Does the defender brake, plant, gather, or overstride? Does body orientation allow a useful next movement? |
| Initial contact | Where is the first actual shoulder/body/arm point? Is contact square or off-center? Is there a meaningful collision impulse? |
| Wrap / restraint | One arm or two? Torso or thigh/leg? Is the grip changing position or visibly slipping? |
| Ground support | Which feet are planted? Does the tackler drive, slide, rotate, fall, or regain footing? Is the runner still stepping? |
| Finish | Did forward velocity stop, direction change, balance fail, the ball go out, or assistance arrive? Was progress ruled stopped? |

### Five principles to remember

- **Impulse:** a useful tackle alters runner momentum; force magnitude alone never tells the whole story.
- **Geometry:** contact points and the runner’s path determine leverage and rotational consequences.
- **Support:** cleats and body alignment allow a defender to exert force beyond the initial collision.
- **Continuity:** securing a wrap and completing a finish are separate challenges.
- **Context:** mass, speed, opponent movement, other defenders, rules and safety affect the result.

> **Key principle:** Most useful one-sentence summary: tackling is a dynamic contest of momentum, contact geometry, available ground reaction, and continuous balance recovery.
