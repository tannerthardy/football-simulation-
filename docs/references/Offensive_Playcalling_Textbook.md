# THE OFFENSIVE PLAYCALLING SYSTEM
## An advanced field manual for game plans, formations, motion, play sequencing, quarterback reads, and the language of modern Saturday and Sunday football

**Research and coaching reference edition — October 2026. Volume VIII in the independent football mechanics series.**

> **Central claim:** Offensive playcalling is not the search for a magic play. It is the management of personnel, defenders' rules, time, space, information, and execution risk. A coordinator assembles a small number of well-taught concepts into many presentations, identifies what the defense is willing to concede, and gives the quarterback a viable answer when the initial picture is wrong.

**Scope and evidence.** This is an independent synthesis of publicly available coaching material, official rules, and football analysis, supported by original teaching diagrams and hypothetical installations. It is not a recovered NFL or college team playbook. Coaches do not use one standard vocabulary: 'Doubles,' 'Ace,' 'Yankee,' 'Stick,' 'Scat,' 'Liz,' and numbered formations can mean different things in different systems. Every worked call in this manual is explicitly an **illustrative teaching call**, not a claim about a particular team's private terminology or assignment. Prototypical quarterback reads are teaching options; there is no universal progression for a concept. NCAA, NFL, high-school, and other codes differ on motion, eligibility, downfield linemen, timing, and formation rules. Check the applicable rulebook. [R1][R2][R3]

**Diagram key.** Offense is shown advancing **up** the page. X/Z/H/Y/F denote example eligibles, C/G/T interior linemen, QB quarterback, and RB/F backs. Colored routes show representative timing/geometry, not precise guaranteed NFL route depths. Numbers (#1, #2, #3) count pass threats from the sideline inward on a side. An outlined defender is a *read key*, not a promise that every defense assigns him identically.

# PART I — HOW A COORDINATOR BUILDS AND CALLS AN OFFENSE

## 1. The operating system: people, plays, answers, and information

There are four interconnected layers to an offensive call: **identity** (what the offense executes well), **presentation** (what the defense sees), **decision** (what the quarterback and blockers do against the actual look), and **counter** (what prevents the defense from overplaying the first three). A playbook is an inventory. A game plan is a selective hypothesis about a specific opponent. A call sheet is a live decision interface. A quarterback's execution card is the tiny set of rules needed to make a complex concept usable under time pressure.

A complete illustrative play can be decomposed into **personnel → formation → shift → motion → run/pass concept → direction or strength → protection → route tags → tempo or snap mechanic → alert/kill rule**. Some systems encode the same information in a three-word phrase; others employ long West Coast-style calls, wristband numbers, sideline signs, or one-word packages. The point is not verbosity: it is that every affected player can recover his assignment and knows which features may change. In film study, do not infer an actual internal call from the announcer's shorthand.

At higher levels, the play caller is solving an optimization problem under imperfect information. A play has an estimated distribution of gains and turnovers, not one destiny. The coordinator knows game situation, available personnel, observed defensive tendencies, the offense's own quality of execution, and what has already been presented on the field. They do **not** know the defense's call. Good playcalling chooses a sound action from a credible set while preserving future options. It must also minimize mental-load, substitution, protection, penalty, and negative-play costs.

### 1.1 How the staff divides the work

An NFL or major-college offensive staff can divide opponent study across the run game, passing game, protections and pressures, third down, red zone, fronts, blitzes, and self-scout. Assistants may supply route or run concepts while the coordinator or head coach chooses the final menu. The quarterback, offensive line coach, running backs coach, and position coaches all test whether the drawing survives actual spacing and execution. Reporting on Bruce Arians' 2015 Cardinals documented a collaborative weekly process, including a QB selecting preferred passes for the opening menu; it is an instructive example, **not** a claim that every staff works the same way. [R4]

| Job | Film-room product | On-field consequence |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Coordinator / play caller | Priority problems and complementary calls | The plan has an identity and fallback |
| Run-game coordinator / OL | Front matrix, fit stress, front movement rules | Blocking landmarks remain sound |
| Pass-game coordinator / QB coach | Coverage matrix, progressions, timing | QB knows reads and answers |
| Protection / blitz staff | Pressure map and hot/scan rules | Free rushers are accounted for or declared |
| WR / TE / RB coaches | Release, route, blocking and motion details | Formational variety does not destroy timing |
| Quality control / analysts | Personnel, formation, situation and self-scout data | Tendencies become hypotheses, not myths |

### 1.2 Offensive identity is not predictability

An offense can feature wide zone and still have answers against reduced fronts, inside penetration, split-safety run-support structures, man-pressure, and late rotation. The coordinator should know which **two or three base concepts** the offense can execute even when the defense expects them, which complements protect those base plays, and which situational packages are necessary. An offense with 150 named calls but only 20 routinely practiced may be less flexible than one with 40 concepts and 100 formation presentations. USA Football's game-planning guidance emphasizes how practice repetitions constrain menu size. [R3]

**Coaching question:** For every call, ask: 'What defender or rule are we stressing? What will it look like if the opponent refuses to give us that answer? What do we do next?' If the coordinator cannot describe both the desired defensive reaction and the contingency, the call is poorly integrated.

## 2. The weekly game-planning cycle: observation to installation

The workflow varies with game day and practice rules, but a representative structure is: review the previous game and self-scout, build a personnel/front/coverage report, choose a small list of attack points, design offensive packages, install those packages in practices, evaluate which plays actually function, then finalize the call sheet and communication process. A play may look excellent on an opponent cut-up but fail against the scout-team representation because the offensive tackle cannot reach the target landmark or a receiver cannot execute the timing. That failure is information, not merely poor effort.

### 2.1 Build the opponent's *response map*, not a bag of coverage percentages

Chart by meaningful context: personnel, offensive formation, down/distance, field/boundary, motion direction, back offset, down-and-distance, score, quarter, red zone, and offense's run/pass threat. Compare **initial shell**, **snap structure**, **post-snap distribution**, and **rush count**. Record sample sizes, opponent quality, and whether the tendency persists after the offensive formation changes. A defense may appear to be '70% split safety' but spin to one-high against under-center 12 personnel or rotate when a detached Y motions into the core.

Study **defensive adjustments**, not only labels. Does the defense bump coverage with motion, run a defender across, rotate the safeties, exchange the nickel/linebacker, or hold the shell and adjust only fits? Does a three-technique follow the back? Who has the edge against wing insert? Does the boundary corner play cloud/trap against condensed #1? Where do mugged linebackers drop if they do not rush? Who handles the third eligible in trips? Those are actionable questions for formation and concept design.

### 2.2 Build a problem–evidence–call matrix

| Opponent evidence | Test call | What would confirm the hypothesis? | If the defense adjusts |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Nickel overfits fast motion | Jet bluff → Counter away | LB displacement and backside fit delay | Use stationary counter when they stop bumping |
| Safety caps deep over, curl-flat widens | Dig under vertical clearout | Intermediate hook lane opens | Throw checkdown or call backside isolation |
| Tite 4i fronts close B gaps | Duo, insert, pin-pull or outside stretch | Interior double teams / edge displacement | Attack rotated overhang with play-action |
| Man vs empty, DB travels with RB | Choice/option route or motion to mismatch | LB/S/N matchup exposed | Trips zone answers if defense checks to zone |
| Nickel pressure follows back offset | Change back alignment and scan / slide | Repeatable pressure side | Screen, quick game or max protection |

Do not trust 'coverage beaters' in the abstract. A good defense has pressures, bracket tags, route exchanges, and leverage adjustments. Test *the actual player rules*, including the defender tasked with run support, rather than only a diagram's three deep zones.

### 2.3 Organize practice around the decisions the quarterback must make

If the install says 'Sail beats Cover 3,' the quarterback learns a brittle slogan. Instead teach the route-spacing landmarks, the curl-flat and deep-third relationships, the footwork, the timing of a safety rotation, and where the ball goes when the defense rotates away from the expected structure. Practice the concept against three credible counterlooks, not a stationary dummy version of the preferred look.

A useful progression is **walkthrough alignment → individual timing and footwork → seven-on-seven route distribution → blitz pickup/team period → situation-specific repetition → noisy/late-motion contingency**. The number of calls matters less than how many relevant branches were practiced. Give the QB deliberate recovery rules: protect ball, go to outlet, throw away when legal, or reset the protection only when within system authority.

## 3. Self-scout: what your own formations tell the defense

Opponents build their game plans around the offense's tendencies. Therefore every formation is also a message to the defense. Audit run/pass rate, target distribution, favorite motion, protection, route concept, and strong/weak tendency *conditional on personnel and situation*. A 65% run rate out of 12 personnel may be meaningful until the offense starts using it almost exclusively on first-and-ten. A supposedly balanced 2x2 shotgun presentation can still telegraph protection direction through the RB offset, reveal screens through WR splits, or reveal a boot through under-center tight-end alignment.

The coordinator must distinguish **bad predictability** from **productive tendency**. Repeating an effective play can be correct if the defense cannot stop it; a tendency is not itself a flaw. Constraints and counters matter when the defense is gaining leverage because it expects that tendency. Self-scout should track *how the defense responds*, then decide whether to exploit that response or simply keep doing the core play.

### 3.1 Data fields that belong in a self-scout log

Record down/distance, hash, personnel, formation, receiver spacing, tight-end side, back offset, under center/gun/pistol, motion/shift, motion-at-snap, front, rotation, run concept, route family, protection, QB decision, first defender contacted, pressure time, result, expected gain, and situation. Avoid treating a four-yard gain on third-and-ten as equivalent to four yards on second-and-three. Combine grading of execution with play-call quality. **A bad outcome is not proof of a bad call, and a touchdown can hide an unsound rule.**

## 4. The game plan menu: translating analysis into a small executable inventory

Use a **base-menu + situation overlays** architecture. Base menus cover open-field early downs and can be called in either direction from multiple formations. Overlays handle third-and-short/medium/long, red zone and goal line, backed-up, two-minute, four-minute, short yardage, unusual pressures, and shot plays. Many teams keep separate plan buckets for two-point plays, end-of-half, overtime, and must-have fourth downs. [R3]

| Situation | Primary question | Typical menu ingredients | Major tradeoff |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| First / second early down | Stay ahead and constrain fits | Core runs, RPOs, play-action, efficient dropback | Explosiveness vs negative plays |
| Second-and-long | Recover efficient distance | Quick game, intermediate, draw, screen | Too conservative vs forced low-percentage shot |
| Third-and-1/2 | Convert against compressed front | Duo, Power, sneaks, fast flat, pick-safe concepts | Penetration / congestion |
| Third-and-3–6 | Beat coverage and pressure at sticks | Stick, choice, spacing, mesh, bunch | Protection/time vs precision |
| Third-and-7+ | Create full-field windows | Dagger, Drive, levels, crossers, screens vs pressure | Sack and turnover exposure |
| High red zone | Vertical space still exists | Shot, screen, run constraints | Windows compress approaching goal |
| Low red zone | Deny leverage in small space | Picks/rubs within rules, bunch, quick outs, fades, runs | Limited depth / tight coverage |
| Backed up | Protect ball and avoid disaster | Strongest runs, quick game, max-secure concepts | Safety, bad field position |
| Four-minute | Keep clock and convert | High-confidence run/protection, selective passes | Predictability and loaded box |
| Two-minute | Clock and sideline constraints | Tempo, fast answers, boundary routes, shot/clock plays | Illegal shift and communication error |

### 4.1 Practice economy

Estimate each new call's **cognitive cost**: new blocking rules, new QB read, unfamiliar motion, different alignment, changed cadence, new eligibility. Re-presenting a familiar concept from a novel formation may be cheaper than introducing a new concept; but only if the formation does not alter the protection or route-spacing details. Treat practice repetitions as scarce resources. An elegant chalkboard answer that cannot be installed is not a viable Sunday call.

## 5. Call sheets, opening scripts, and decision support

A call sheet normally separates *callable groups* rather than listing plays alphabetically. Practical views include openers, open-field first/second down, key runs, quick passes, play-action, shots, screen, movement, third down by distance, low red zone, goal line, backed-up, two-minute, four-minute, alerts/checks, and adjustments. The sheet should expose both **what the coordinator wants** and **what changed**. Each cell might include personnel, formation, concept, direction, protection, QB alert, and an annotation about the defensive look to avoid.

An opening script is a prepared plan for how to begin probing the opponent—not an obligation to call plays in order regardless of circumstances. Its functions include settling player execution, forcing the defense to declare responses to several formations, seeing answers to motion, showing run/play-action relationships, and identifying whether the defensive plan differs from film. Bill Walsh's 'first fifteen' influence is well documented, but public accounts show implementation varies. The 2015 Cardinals account described Arians choosing a menu of initial runs while Carson Palmer selected preferred opening passes. [R4]

### 5.1 An illustrative twelve-call opening menu

| Call | Presentation | Football purpose | Diagnostic question |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | 11, 2x2 gun | Inside zone | Box fit / shade and overhang? |
| 2 | 11, 3x1 | Stick or spacing | Apex width and pressure? |
| 3 | 12, condensed, motion | Wide zone | Who carries motion? Who sets edge? |
| 4 | Same family | Boot flood | Does backside edge chase? Is flat replaced? |
| 5 | 11, bunch | Mesh | Man switch/banjo or zone match? |
| 6 | 11, static | Duo | Will ILB trigger downhill against doubles? |
| 7 | 12, Y-trade | Counter | Do front and safety reset to new surface? |
| 8 | 11, gun | Dagger | Does middle safety cap seam? |
| 9 | 21, tight | Power | How does base personnel fit extra gap? |
| 10 | 11, empty | Quick choice | Who follows RB from backfield? |
| 11 | 12, same wide-zone look | Yankee shot with max protection | Is post/over window present? |
| 12 | 11, compressed | Screen / orbit return | Does force defender run with motion? |

This is an **illustrative test sequence**. It must be re-ordered by actual field position, down/distance, ball-security needs, QB comfort, and in-game answers. In particular, do not assume a shot play will be called on third-and-long or backed up just because its number appears on a list. A useful script is a decision tree with priorities and replacement calls.

## 6. Play sequencing: how calls create each other's advantages

A coordinator sequences three different things: **the defense's expectations**, **the physical location of defenders**, and **the defender's response rules**. Repeated wide zone might move linebackers horizontally; a bootleg then exploits a backside edge that chases the run. A bubble screen forces the nickel to widen or a corner to trigger; a later inside run uses the changed overhang geometry. A speed motion can identify man/zone rules; a return motion can punish a defense that over-rotates. A tight split can disguise a deep over, screen, crack, or insert block from a similar stance.

Crucially, a coordinator should distinguish **constraint**, **complement**, and **counter**. A constraint discourages a defender from cheating (read keeper behind an end crashing the back). A complement reuses a similar look to attack another area (wide-zone boot). A counter exploits the opponent's learned response (counter away from aggressive jet support). These categories overlap, but they sharpen the play caller's thinking.

### 6.1 A sequencing decision tree

1. **What just happened?** Record defensive personnel, front, rotation, pressure, and who won the leverage battle—not merely yards.
2. **Why?** Was the issue front penetration, a bad fit, offensive technique, negative leverage, or an unaccounted defender?
3. **What has the opponent now been shown?** The same formation can carry a different constraint. Note which defender has begun anticipating it.
4. **What is the next cheapest stress?** Change one ingredient at a time where possible: motion, formation, same play opposite direction, adjacent concept, or protection.
5. **When should the offense return to the base play?** If a counter changes how the defense responds, the base may become valuable again.

A good sequence can still fail because a guard loses one-on-one or a QB misses a throw. Do not rewrite the entire plan after one variance-heavy result. Conversely, a negative play may reveal a recurring protection problem that merits an immediate structural fix.

## 7. In-game adaptation: the headset loop

Once the game starts, coaches exchange a fast stream of observations. The booth has an elevated view of safety rotation, linebacker fits, route distributions, and pattern-match responses. The sideline can judge player condition, execution, front mechanics, and communication. Depending on rules and available technology, photos, tablets, or other approved tools help confirm. The play caller should rely on **short diagnostic language**: 'Boundary safety inserts on Y trade'; '3-tech follows back'; 'nickel rush / end drop'; 'motion no longer travels'; 'weak hook opens versus fast #3.'

The adjustment process is **observe → classify → confirm → recommend → call → teach**. Before adopting a major change, check if the event is repeatable. A single corner traveling with a receiver may be a man-coverage indicator, a match tag, or a disguised zone check. Do not send the quarterback into an unfavorable call because a scout guessed wrong from one screenshot.

### 7.1 Halftime triage

Rank fixes by impact and install cost. (A) **Immediate rules:** tighter protection declaration, altered route landmark, changed motion timing. (B) **Presentation adjustments:** same concepts in formation families exposing different defenders. (C) **Menu replacement:** remove a concept consistently vulnerable to a particular front or coverage. (D) **Emergency package:** if normal protections are consistently outnumbered, use max protection, quick game, move QB, or establish a different run threat. Be suspicious of introducing a complicated new route conversion that was never practiced.

# PART II — FORMATIONS, SHIFTS, MOTIONS, AND DEFENSIVE MANIPULATION

## 8. The legal formation grammar: alignment precedes tactics

Before drawing a clever motion, the coordinator must build a **legal eligible-receiver distribution**. In standard NFL scrimmage formations, seven or more offensive players must be on the line; among usual eligible-number players, the end players on the line can be eligible, while an eligible player covered by another teammate on the line becomes ineligible for a forward pass by his alignment. Backfield players can be eligible subject to rule-specific numbering/reporting conditions. The NFL limits moving players at the snap and requires a one-second simultaneous set following a shift involving two or more players. The exact definitions and reporting exceptions matter; consult the current NFL Rule 7, not a chalkboard shortcut. [R1]

**Personnel** answers *who* is on the field, not how those players line up. A 12-personnel package (one back, two tight ends) can be two tight ends attached to an under-center formation, one detached as a slot, both detached in empty, or placed in a wing/offset alignment. A 21 package may feature a fullback leading from I, aligned as a wing, or motioning out to a receiver position. A wide receiver can align in the backfield as a legal motion player. Multiple presentations tax defensive declaration while preserving offensive assignment familiarity.

### 8.1 A robust formation naming model

I recommend recording every snap as a structured tuple:

`personnel | QB location | eligible distribution | core surfaces | splits | back location | strength | motion / shift | eligible line status`

Example teaching notation: `12 | gun | 2x2 | Y attached right / U wing left | reduced X | RB left | motion U across → snap`. This prevents a game-film label such as 'Trips Right' from silently hiding an attached tight end or ineligible covered player. For coordinators and a simulation engine alike, names are aliases on top of actual geometry.

### 8.2 Split geometry is a tactical variable

A WR aligned at the top of the numbers and another aligned a yard outside the tackle can both run a 'post,' but their paths through underneath defenders differ radically. A wide split compresses the defender's available outside field and can create isolated sidelines. A nasty/reduced split gives a WR room to run deep overs, crossers, crack on force players, or access the opposite hash. Condensed formations can force corners to play unfamiliar run-support or banjo rules and complicate pattern-match distribution. Their increased NFL use is documented, with The Ringer reporting an increase from 11% of NFL offensive snaps in 2016 to 38.5% in 2025 using Next Gen Stats definitions. [R5]

## 9. Personnel and core surfaces: 10/11/12/13/20/21/22/23 and beyond

The two-digit personnel convention usually means **running backs, then tight ends**, with remaining offensive skill players as receivers in an 11-man grouping: 11 is one RB and one TE, 12 is one RB and two TEs, 21 is two RBs and one TE, 10 is one RB and no TE, and so on. The second back need not function as an old-fashioned fullback. Teams differ in whether they label a hybrid as RB, TE, or WR; chart actual on-field identities and roles, not only the roster position label. Quarterback is not included in those two digits.

| Package | Eligible skill composition | Typical strategic utility | Typical defensive conflict |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 10 | 1 RB / 0 TE / 4 WR | Four-wide spacing, fast matchup stress | Light box vs quick game / verticals |
| 11 | 1 RB / 1 TE / 3 WR | Full spread-to-compact menu | Nickel must handle both fit and slot |
| 12 | 1 RB / 2 TE / 2 WR | Extra gaps, balanced PA / empty flexibility | Base personnel vs detached TE speed |
| 13 | 1 RB / 3 TE / 1 WR | Heavy surfaces and condensed threats | Front loading vs play-action shots |
| 20 | 2 RB / 0 TE / 3 WR | Two-back options and motion | Two potential protection / route backs |
| 21 | 2 RB / 1 TE / 2 WR | Lead runs, split flow, misdirection | Extra insert blocker vs safety support |
| 22 | 2 RB / 2 TE / 1 WR | Heavy run, condensed PA | Defending extra gaps and throwback |
| 23 | 2 RB / 3 TE / 0 WR | Heavy and goal-line tendencies | Eligibility and extra run surfaces |
| 01 | 0 RB / 1 TE / 4 WR | Spread passing with a Y | Empty vs disguised pressure |
| 02 | 0 RB / 2 TE / 3 WR | Detached Y/U in empty | Nickel/linebacker mismatch choices |

### 9.1 Y-on, Y-off, wing, nub, and unbalanced problems

An **attached Y** is typically on or near the line next to the tackle, extending the blocking surface. A **Y-off/wing** is behind or off the line near the tackle, able to insert, slice, arc, or motion. A **nub** usually presents a single attached TE as the entire eligible surface to one side (no outside WR) and can distort defensive run/pass strength. An **unbalanced line** changes the line distribution and requires eligibility accounting; defensive fronts may adjust their gap count rather than merely flip personnel. Moving a Y from attached right to wing left can change run strength, edge count, force leverage, and the passing strength simultaneously.

### 9.2 Why the same personnel matters from empty

Going from 12 heavy to 12 empty forces a defense to choose: keep base linebackers against detached tight ends, substitute nickel/dime and risk run fits if the offense returns to condensed without changing players, or play a coverage match that asks linebackers to carry a route they rarely handle. The offense can only profit if its tight ends can align and run those concepts competently, and if the QB has a coherent pressure answer with no RB in the backfield.

## 10. Motion and shift taxonomy: from decoration to coercion

A **shift** changes offensive alignment and, under NFL rules, typically requires the entire affected formation to become set for a full second after multiple players move. **Motion** generally means one eligible backfield player moving before the snap; in the NFL that player may be in motion at the snap within the prescribed legal direction limits. End-on-line receivers moving off and forward movement toward the line introduce illegal-motion and illegal-shift risks. College and other rules differ, and special formations/quarterback provisions matter. [R1]

The rise is measurable: The Ringer reported NFL pre-snap motion usage increasing from **46.2% of snaps in 2018 to 63.3% in 2025**, using Next Gen Stats categories. That is historical context, not a claim about the unfinished 2026 season. [R5]

Motion serves at least six distinct functions: (1) **information probe** (who responds?); (2) **leverage creation** (change WR access to contact); (3) **defensive relocation** (move a nickel or safety); (4) **run-fit manipulation** (change force, extra gap or insert angle); (5) **tempo and timing** (arrive with speed or create backfield misdirection); (6) **tendency management** (same presentation, different result). A coordinator should identify the intended purpose of motion, rather than calling it because it looks modern.

### 10.1 Common forms and their physics

| Term | Basic path | Typical football use | Operational risk |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Jet / fly | Fast transverse across core | Sweep threat, leverage test, false flow | Snap timing / running into exchange |
| Orbit | Backward arc behind QB | Reverse, screen, option, rotation stress | Long developing path |
| Return / yo-yo | Motion away then reverse | Disrupt bump rules; deceive flow | Illegal shift / timing |
| Zip / short / across | Receiver moves to reduced or opposite alignment | Change # count and blocking angle | Crowd, coverage recheck |
| Trade | TE shifts from one side to other | Change run strength/front check | Must satisfy set requirements |
| Yo / Y-motion | TE wing to attach or detach | Extra gap or seam release | Snap assignment miscommunication |
| Slide / glide | Receiver motion parallel just before snap | Running start without changing call | Route timing / legality |
| Fast / cheat | Motion receiver rapidly toward a release landmark | Avoid press and steal leverage | QB cadence precision |
| Push / exit | Slot moves outward | Change spacing / receiver numbers | Eligibility at snap |
| Insert / sift | Wing or H relocates toward core | Trap, split zone, protection | Edge collision / fit re-count |
| RB out / empty | Back motions to detached alignment | Reveal coverage and eliminate backfield blocker | Hot / protection redesign |
| Short motion to stack | WR creates close two-man set | Banjo/press avoidance | Pick rules / legal set |

Terminology is intensely team-specific; the table describes families, not universally standardized names. A common coaching trick is a **same destination, different path** package: full jet, short zip, and orbit can all finish near an H-back landmark but solicit different defensive reactions because the path and timing differed.

## 11. How motions and shifts actually manipulate defensive rules

**Run-fit manipulation:** a defense may bump linebackers and overhangs toward jet, rotate a safety, or exchange outside force. If the motion causes the backside linebacker to travel, Counter away or an interior insert can exploit the delay. If the defense holds its box rules and rotates only the secondary, the same attack may be worthless. Therefore measure **which defender moved, how far, and whether he was displaced when the ball hit the line**. The ball carrier cannot gain from an imagined motion bonus.

**Coverage-rule manipulation:** in a 2x2 quarters structure, a nickel might relate to #2 and a safety read #2. When the #2 receiver fast-motions across the formation, both sides may require revised number declarations or an emergency 'push' rule. Motion into trips may provoke a Poach/Special/Stubbie check. Motion away from trips can leave the weak-side safety and nickel with different responsibilities. A fast-out motion can instantly create a new widest receiver at the snap; defenses using route-numbering rules must account for him. The Ringer's 2024 examination of motion responses highlights the demand placed on defensive communication. [R6]

**Matchup manipulation:** if a corner follows the motion, that is evidence of a travel/man-related responsibility but not proof of Cover 1. It could be a tagged match or bracket adjustment. If the defense bumps without pursuit, it could be zone, switch-man, or a rule-based match check. The quarterback should carry hypotheses into the snap instead of treating one response as a guaranteed answer.

**Protection manipulation:** moving the RB changes the offense's available scan protector and can change the defense's pressure presentation. If the back leaves into empty, the line and QB must agree which potential rusher is no longer blocked. Returning the back late can force the defense to communicate a new rush or coverage matchup, but it also strains offensive timing and protection adjustments.

### 11.1 The motion-to-run-fit matrix

| Offensive change | Defensive choice | Potential offensive response | Must verify |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Slot jets across | LB bumps / safety rotates | Counter or split zone away | Who is force and cutback? |
| TE trades from right to left | Front reduces/flips | Run back into over-shift | Gap count and OL IDs |
| RB motions to empty | LB follows | Isolate RB on option route | Hot defender left by RB departure |
| Wing orbits behind QB | Edge widens / nickel flows | Inside zone or keep underneath | Edge still controls QB? |
| Condensed stack becomes wide | DBs fan / bump | Run at light apex or quick access | How coverage numbers re-form |

### 11.2 Motion timing is not free

Early motion allows the defense to adjust calmly and may reveal assignments; late motion may exploit timing but increase the chance of an offensive illegal shift, route error, mistimed snap, or missed protection declaration. The play caller should choose motion timing deliberately. A noisy road game may require a visual or tactile cadence system and a simpler script; a late-return motion without a coherent snap mechanic can hurt the offense more than the defense.

## 12. Bunch, stack, condensed splits, empty, and formation variants

**Bunch** is a tight three-player cluster; **stack** places two eligible threats near the same line of attack, one behind another; **compressed** formations reduce receiver splits; **empty** removes the conventional back from behind the QB. All four complicate *defensive sorting*: the defense must identify which eligible becomes outermost, who takes the first fast-out release, whether man defenders switch, whether corners play press/bail, and where run-support players align. The offense must simultaneously manage legal alignments and avoid offensive pass interference when routes naturally cross.

Bunch allows **point releases** that shield one player from jam and generate controlled traffic. Route families include bunch flood, switch releases, mesh, stack option, snag, and corner/flat. Against a good banjo or box defense, however, clustered releases can be distributed cleanly; bunch is not an automatic man-coverage winner. A condensed X can run a deep over, crack block, return route, insert for run support, or throwback wheel in the same pre-snap presentation.

### 12.1 Same concept, four presentations

Consider a **Snag/Spot** family: a corner, a short spot/hook, and a flat-control route stress a three-player zone triangle. It can be run from normal 2x2, a three-man bunch, 3x1 with the RB to the flat, or an attached Y with a motioning F. The QB's *conflict defender and timing* might remain familiar while the offense changes the defender's pre-snap declaration and the route release geometry. But an unfamiliar formation might change who clears the corner route and must be treated as a genuine teaching variation.

## 13. Tempo, audibles, kills, and the QB's menu at the line

A quarterback may be given a **one-play call**, a **run/pass paired call**, an **alert** (throw a specific matchup if present), a **kill/can** (switch between two pre-called plays under a stated trigger), a **check-with-me** (look to sideline after defensive alignment), or a true audible system changing protection and routes. Distinguish each level of authority. If the QB can change *everything* on every snap, practice must cover an enormous state space; if he cannot change anything, the offense risks walking into unblocked pressure or a disastrous box count.

Tempo likewise has variants: **huddle, sugar huddle, no-huddle, NASCAR/fast, check-with-me, freeze, muddle, and two-minute clock**. Fast tempo can restrict substitutions, simplify the defensive call, and prevent specialized personnel; it can also limit offensive shifts, make protection harder to verify, and reduce the coordinator's access to a rich play-call language. Freeze cadence can test whether the defense exposes rotation or pressure, but play-clock management and legal substitution mechanics still govern.

### 13.1 A practical QB hierarchy

1. **Security:** Does the offense have a legal formation and the necessary blockers for the predicted immediate threats? Who is unblocked by design?
2. **Structure:** Where are the safeties, defenders over #2/#3, and the apex; how many defenders are in the tackle box?
3. **Matchup:** Can the called concept exploit cushion, leverage, or alignment without abandoning protection?
4. **Authority:** Is there an authorized alert, kill, or check? Does the trigger *actually* exist or is it a disguised trap?
5. **Post-snap confirmation:** Expect the shell to change. Execute the installed branch rather than clinging to a presnap guess.

Good offenses simplify this ladder into a short pre-snap mnemonic so a QB can complete it under the play clock, stadium noise, and physical pressure.

# PART III — RUN GAME, PROTECTION, SCREENS, AND COMPLEMENTARY DESIGN

## 14. Run-family architecture: zone, gap, man, option, and influence

Offensive run concepts can be grouped by how they create space and control defenders. **Zone schemes** coordinate area and movement-based blocking with read/aiming-point principles; **gap schemes** often use down blocks, pullers and insert blockers to create favorable angles; **man/duo styles** emphasize controlled double teams and vertical displacement; **option schemes** intentionally leave a defender unblocked or read him; **influence/trap schemes** invite penetration into a controlled block angle. These categories overlap in actual installations. A team may run inside zone with man tags or use a gap-style insert off zone footwork.

### 14.1 Run-concept scouting reference

| Concept | Typical OL approach | RB/coordinator target | Defensive answer / counter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Inside zone | Playside zone combinations; read DL/LB movement | Press track, identify frontside/backside crease | Slants, penetration, scrape exchanges |
| Wide/outside zone | Horizontal stretch, reach/overtake, backside pursuit cutoff | Press outside landmark; cut or bounce when leverage declares | Edges anchor/box, LB fast flow |
| Duo | Powerful double teams and vertical displacement | Read LB fit through double teams | Defenders spill/penetrate, stacked LB fits |
| Power | Down blocks, kick-out/log and insert/wrap | Follow insert into designed interior leverage | Wrong-arm, spill/box and filling LB |
| Counter GT/GH | Down blocks and backside pullers | Counter steps then kick/wrap read | Penetration, scrape exchange, false-step discipline |
| Trap / Wham | Influence defender, unexpected angled block | Hit immediate crease behind trap | DL squeezes/recognizes trap |
| Pin-and-pull | Down/pin rules with pullers in space | Attack perimeter angle | Force defender maintains outside leverage |
| Split zone | Zone flow plus H slice across back side | Zone read with controlled cutback | Edge squeeze, exchange or H collision |
| Draw | Pass set influence then delayed interior run | Allow rush to widen, then attack space | DL maintains rush-lane discipline |
| Toss/crack | Perimeter leverage and receiver cracks | Race to edge with support blockers | Corner/overhang triggers, force exchange |
| Read option / zone read | Read selected defender while others zone block | QB/RB decision tied to read key | Scrape exchange or disciplined mesh defense |
| Bash | RB and QB tracks invert typical read-option picture | Stress fits with QB interior / RB perimeter | LB/edge responsibility exchange |
| Veer/arc | Option or arc block changes force account | Read or block next-level threat | Alley/safety fits and pitch discipline |

### 14.2 A run is not defined solely by its named gap

A coordinator may call Power toward the right C gap, but the final lane depends on defensive force techniques, the puller's arrival, whether the edge spills or boxes, and where the RB sets up blocks. The offensive design should teach kick-or-log contingencies and the runner's response. In zone runs, 'bang/bend/bounce' is a useful coaching mnemonic in some systems, not an algorithm that overrides the actual defender fit. A player who breaks the intended track before the blocks form can ruin the leverage created by the scheme.

## 15. Run calls as defensive tests: numbers, surfaces, and force

The play caller should map **tackle-box count**, **the extra gap created by an attached Y or motioning wing**, and **the force/alley players**. A six-man box against 11 personnel is not automatically favorable if an apex/safety is the seventh run fitter and can trigger unblocked. A six-man box against QB run can be short a defender because the quarterback is the additional ball carrier. Conversely, a seven-man box can be vulnerable if a detached receiver holds the safety responsible for a quick glance.

### 15.1 The attached-Y run menu

Start with Y attached right and a reduced X. Use (1) wide zone to move the front, (2) split zone to handle backside pursuit, (3) duo/inside zone to attack a widened end, (4) Power/Counter when a second puller creates angles, (5) boot/flood off the zone track, (6) play-action over/post when the middle support inserts. This is not a magical sequencing ladder; each call tests a *different defender behavior* while preserving a recognizable family of OL footwork and backfield action.

### 15.2 Defenses can defeat the plan by refusing to chase ghosts

A disciplined defense may exchange run fits without over-rotating to motion, squat on a boot flat while the edge contains, or show a two-high shell while fitting an eight-man run picture late. Your game plan should include **reliable base runs** even when constraint plays do not create obvious leverage. Avoid assuming the defense must respond to a fake. Film study should grade how credible the fake looks given OL behavior, QB mesh and protection, not simply the play name.

## 16. Play-action, movement passes, and holding defensive conflict

**Play-action** uses simulated run action to influence second-level eyes, rush discipline, and coverage spacing. It can be paired with under-center wide zone, downhill duo/power, shotgun mesh action, split flow, reverse action, or a run-pass conflict. It does **not** always require successful rushing in the same game; the quality of the backfield action, offensive line presentation, down/distance, and defenders' assigned reads matter. A coordinator should not automatically remove play-action from the menu after several unproductive runs.

**Boot** moves the QB away from the original run action and often combines a deep or intermediate crossing route, a flat/slide threat, and a clearout. A **naked** leaves a particular edge unblocked by design and requires a specific keep/throw plan. A **keeper** has the QB retain the ball on movement; 'boot,' 'keeper,' and 'waggle' are used differently across systems. **Sprint-out/rollout** relocates the launch point more directly, potentially reducing full-field reading while changing rush geometry.

### 16.1 The movement-pass stress test

Against a disciplined force player, the offense must decide whether an H will sift across to protect the edge, whether the QB has a throwaway lane, and whether the flat receiver can settle before a cloud corner attacks. Against a defense exchanging responsibilities with a safety, the deeper over route may be covered even though the front pursued the fake. The QB should be given a clear **edge-danger rule** (abort/get ball out) and **flat-to-intermediate** or **alert-to-progression** concept, rather than drifting indefinitely in the hope that coverage dissolves.

## 17. Screens, RPOs, and constraints: forcing the unblocked defender to choose

Screens exploit spacing, aggression and blocking landmarks. Common families: **RB slow/fast screen, middle screen, slip screen, TE screen, bubble, now/smoke, tunnel, jailbreak, orbit/toss screen, and swing screen**. Screen success depends on the position of force, the eligible screen blocker and his approach angle, the ball's timing relative to downfield linemen, and how the defense triggers. A call should designate *which blocker takes which pursuer*, not merely 'get in space.' Against a cloud corner who triggers immediately, a bubble may be a poor gift even when the nickel vacates.

**RPO** is an offense where a live run path and a throwing option coexist through a post-snap read. It requires precise protections and rule compliance. A **pre-snap access throw** selected before the play begins is not necessarily the same as a post-snap RPO. Read-option, triple option, and RPO differ in which player and phase are being read. Common packaged throws include glance, slant, bubble, hitch, stick, pop/seam, and fast screen. College and NFL ineligible-downfield rules have different consequences for how long a QB may hold a read; do not import a college install unchanged into the NFL. [R1]

### 17.1 Example: Inside zone + backside glance

Define the linebacker as the conflict defender. He has a run-fit responsibility and a passing-lane responsibility; the offense wants to make him choose. If he inserts downhill into the intended fit, the QB may throw a glance behind his vacated window **only if** the safety/corner leverage and protection rules permit. If he hangs under the glance, the RB takes the handoff into the intended fit. This is a **teaching rule**, not a blanket promise: a safety can poach the route, the defensive end can scrape exchange, or the backside corner can take the glance with inside leverage. The QB must not throw into a covered window merely because the linebacker moved.

## 18. Protection is part of playcalling, not an unrelated OL detail

A passing concept is only viable if the quarterback can reach the intended read before pressure arrives. The offensive staff must know whether the line is in **full-slide, half-slide, man/BOB, six-man with RB, seven-man/max, empty five-man, sprint-out**, or another system. The **Mike/point declaration** is often a counting reference, not the identity of the true middle linebacker. In a half-slide concept, the slide side handles gaps and the man side works its assigned bodies; the RB may scan away from the slide. Against six threats, a five-man protection must designate who is hot or otherwise accounted for by the QB and receiver.

### 18.1 Protection changes the pass concept's value

Dagger and Yankee can demand a longer hold for deeper routes; a six- or seven-man protection reduces immediate route threats and may help the QB achieve that time. Quick stick and slant can be viable against pressure but still fail if a mugged defender drops into the throwing lane. Boot changes the launch point but creates edge-risk and half-field spacing limitations. Empty forces a very explicit account of the defense's sixth potential rusher. **Protection cannot be evaluated from the front alone; the QB's release timing is part of the protection plan.**

### 18.2 Sample pre-snap protection matrix

| Defensive picture | Primary concern | Offensive check / call design | QB fallback |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Four-down, wide nine | Edge arrival, interior one-on-one | Set angles; chip / 6th protector when needed | Climb/reset, outlet |
| Tite/Mint 4i-0-4i | Interior squeeze and edge pressure threats | Count edges; handle 4i movement | Throw into vacated replacement if confirmed |
| Double-A mug | Which LB comes/drops; overload | Clear point/slide/back scan | Know exact hot lane, not assumed blitz |
| Field nickel pressure | Back/slot side assignment | Slide or fast counter, pressure indicator | Quick timing / alert |
| Zero pressure | More rushers than protection | Max secure, hot, sight, throwaway rules | Ball out before free rusher arrives |
| Simulated pressure | Unexpected rusher and dropping DL | Scan actual rush, avoid preordained reads | Re-identify dropping defender post-snap |

### 18.3 Why 'the defense blitzed' is not enough analysis

Grade a pressure failure by: poor point, unsupported protection, bad individual technique, missed exchange, RB scan error, QB late decision, or a deliberately free rusher not handled by the QB. A play caller may fix the first with a check, the second with concept/protection pairing, and the last with different timing. Do not change offensive identity because an isolated guard was beaten on a correctly blocked call.

# PART IV — PASS DESIGN AND THE QUARTERBACK'S DECISION PROCESS

## 19. The quarterback's pre-snap problem: distinguish appearance, structure, and threat

The quarterback can reliably observe **alignment**, not the defense's hidden call. Start with offensive formation and available eligibles, then locate potential rushers, safety depth, corner leverage, nickel/apex alignment, and linebacker stack or mug presentations. Count defenders with a plausible immediate rush path. Look for corner depth variations, weak safety rotation toward trips, unusual depth over a tight-end nub, and coverage mismatches caused by motion. These are **probabilistic clues**; an offense cannot reliably diagnose Cover 3 or quarters from the high safeties alone because two-high presentation can become one-high and vice versa.

A disciplined pre-snap workflow:

1. **Defensive front and rush potential:** identify alignments, edge wideness, overload potential and point-protection constraints.
2. **Middle of field:** is it apparently open (MOFO/two-high) or closed (MOFC/one-high)? Record it as a hypothesis.
3. **Matchup and leverage:** which defender holds inside/outside position against #1/#2/#3? Is a soft corner conceding short access?
4. **Motion information:** did a defender travel, bump, rotate or exchange? What competing explanations fit?
5. **Best answer and clock:** alert only if coverage/pressure requirements are satisfied; otherwise confirm concept and work the installed progression.
6. **Post-snap verification:** identify actual rotation, matching behavior and rush; quickly replace any disproven pre-snap belief.

### 19.1 Do not confuse field landmarks with coverages

A defender 12 yards deep and outside the hash might eventually take a half-field, post, quarter, poach, robber or run-support assignment. A corner playing press might carry man, play cloud, trap #2's out, or bluff pressure. The QB should attend to **movement at the snap** and how defenders respond to routes. 'One-high' is not a guarantee of Cover 1; it can be Cover 3, fire zone, robber or a split-field rotation that briefly resembles one-high.

## 20. Four distinct quarterback reading systems

There is no universal progression for 'Mesh' or 'Flood.' A team's playbook specifies whether the QB reads **an individual defender**, **a progression of eligible receivers**, **a coverage structure**, or **an alert followed by a progression**. Hybrids are extremely common. USA Football coaching interviews document different ways coaches teach Y Cross and progression reads, including variations that begin at backside isolation or a post instead of a generic high-low. [R7][R8][R9]

### 20.1 Pure progression

The QB works a fixed ordered family of reads, shaped by the progression's timing and footwork: e.g., **alert go → first in-breaker → second crosser → RB outlet**. The sequence is not 'stare at everyone'; the drop rhythm establishes when the QB can throw each route. Progression is robust against many coverages but can waste time when an obvious unworked window appears. It must include a *reset trigger* if pressure invalidates the normal read sequence.

### 20.2 Key-defender or conflict read

The QB keys a particular underneath or deep defender whose movement determines which of two routes becomes open. In **Smash**, a corner in the short outside area and a half/quarter safety above may define a high-low that changes with coverage. In **Stick**, the apex can be stressed by stick/sit inside and fast flat outside. In **Sail/Flood**, the flat/curl defender and deep-third safety form a layered read. 'High-low' does not mean the QB literally watches both routes equally; he identifies a player who cannot defend two separated windows at the same moment.

### 20.3 Coverage-confirmation read

The QB reads the post-snap structure and selects a branch such as **MOFC vs MOFO** or **man vs zone** before going through a limited progression. The danger is oversimplification: match coverages can be indistinguishable from man until routes declare, and late rotation can invalidate the initial branch. Better coaching uses **two relevant clues** and an answer that is safe if the identification is uncertain.

### 20.4 Alert-to-progression / choice read

A QB may be coached to take an **alert** against a particular leverage or coverage (for instance an isolated X fade versus no deep safety and outside leverage), but if the alert fails, his feet and eyes return to a specific concept progression. This is not freelancing: alert quality depends on documented coverage, release, timing, and pressure; throws that cannot arrive before help are poor alerts. In option/choice systems, the receiver and quarterback may share leverage rules (sit versus zone, pivot versus leverage, burst versus safety), requiring both players to diagnose the same picture.

### 20.5 Compare read designs on the same route family

| Route concept | One reasonable teaching read | Alternative teaching read | When the first breaks |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Mesh | Alert matchup; cross #1 → cross #2 → sit/outlet | Key underneath cross traffic and leverage | Robber or bracket takes first crosser |
| Y Cross | Vertical alert → cross → flat/outlet | Backside isolation → safety to cross → underneath | Safety poaches cross or boundary X wins fast |
| Dagger | Seam clearout → dig → checkdown | Safety movement/MIKE drop key → dig | Seam defender walls dig route |
| Smash | Read corner/half relationship → hitch/corner | Alert corner then progression backside | Trap corner or rotated safety undercuts hitch |
| Flood/Sail | Deep clearout → intermediate out → flat | Flat-curl high-low, safety confirmed | Cloud/trap corner breaks on flat |
| Four verts | Check middle access / benders → alert seam | Inside-out progression; field side first | Match quarters carries both verticals |
| Stick | Apex key → stick or flat | Alert access hitch, then stick-flat | Apex walls stick; cloud traps flat |
| Drive | Shallow → dig → back | Dig alert if LB jumps shallow | Robber takes dig / free hitter shortens pocket |

These are **example installations**, not canonical rulebooks. QB teaching is evaluated by synchronized route landmarks, the decision trigger, body/eyes and release timing—not whether an announcer can recite a single number progression.

## 21. Modern pass-concept families: the defender each one stresses

A pass concept is a coordinated route structure. Route names indicate approximate geometry, but successful installations teach exact stems, break landmarks, spacing, coverage adjustments, and releases. The same concept may be used with a different backside tag, RB checkdown or protection. Below is a coverage-neutral anatomy of major Saturday/Sunday families.

### 21.1 Quick game

**Stick:** a short in/settle route paired with a flat route and sometimes vertical clear. Stress: apex's inside-vs-outside conflict. QB: confirm apex expansion or wall, make a fast throw with feet on schedule. Vulnerability: a corner trapping flat or match defender passing off correctly.

**Spacing:** receivers occupy multiple shallow zones at measured width; may include two hitches and a central spot. Stress: zone spacing and quick access. QB: scan leverage, throw to the space a defender vacates; against tight man, separation may not materialize merely because routes are 'spread.'

**Snag/Spot:** a three-receiver triangle with spot, corner and flat. Stress: flat/curl defender plus high safety relationship. QB: choose a defined high-low with an alert if the corner is left open; if the corner sits on the flat, the spot route's landmark matters. Requires tight receiver spacing without offensive pick/contact violation.

**Slant/Flat:** inside slant and outside fast-flat route; high-low/horizontal stretch on the apex/curl defender. Against man, releases and leverage decide success; against zone, a dropping defensive lineman can unexpectedly take the slant lane.

**Hitch/Seam, Curl/Flat, All Hitches:** leverage/access families creating rhythm completions or a window under a deeper defender. A hitch against a hard cloud corner is not inherently available. Coach QB ball placement away from the leverage player and account for corner break speed.

**Hoss:** commonly hitch outside and seam inside, potentially with a 'Juke' or choice tag in modern systems. It can stress split-safety seams and underneath spacing, but exact read and tags vary considerably. The QB must know where inside seam help appears and who replaces an apex that widens.

### 21.2 Intermediate crossers and layered concepts

**Mesh:** two shallow crossers intersect with designed depth separation and vertical/settle complements. Against man it creates traffic and leverage difficulty; against zone, a sit route or outlet may become the controlled completion. A legal natural rub is not permission for a receiver to intentionally block an eligible defender on a pass route.

**Drive:** shallow cross beneath a deeper dig or in-breaker; stress underneath linebackers and the second-level wall. QB can evaluate whether the hook defender sinks under the dig or triggers on the shallow. Requires enough protection for the dig to develop.

**Levels:** two or more in-breakers at distinct depths; attack middle-hook depth relationship and distribution. Deep defenders can overlap if QB waits too long; spacing and boundary starting splits matter.

**Dagger:** vertical seam or clearout threatening the high safety, combined with a deeper dig/in-breaker behind underneath defenders. Stress: middle safety and hook defender allocation. If the clearout does not threaten the safety, the dig window may never appear. Matt Bowen's public concept analysis documents the general structure; variants differ. [R10]

**Y Cross / H Cross:** a tight-end/inside receiver crosses the formation at intermediate/deep depth, often paired with vertical and flat-control routes. The receiver should read underneath wall and stay within timing landmarks. Teaching progressions vary: public coaching interviews describe versions beginning with backside X access and others beginning with the post/safety. [R7][R8]

**Shallow Cross:** one shallow crossing receiver, commonly paired with dig or verticals; not automatically two-way Mesh. Against man, crossers use traffic; against zone, QB looks for windows between hook defenders.

### 21.3 Vertical, high-low and shot concepts

**Four Verticals:** four vertical distribution threats, with seam benders and possible outside adjustments; stretch deep safeties and pattern-match rules. QB must know which route can bend away from post or split-safety help and whether the underneath defender carries #3. The called route isn't always a straight 'go' for every receiver. USA Football provides public Air Raid four-vertical variations. [R11]

**Flood/Sail:** routes at deep, intermediate and flat levels toward one side; stress outside third and curl-flat relationships. The QB may have half-field progression but must not drift toward the defender's angle or ignore backside free pressure. In Cover 2 cloud, outside corner may squat under flat and half safety handle deep—so the 'Cover 3 beater' label is incomplete.

**Smash:** short hitch/curl with deeper corner; high-low on corner/half-field support. Various systems tag slot fade or corner changes. The QB should avoid blindly throwing a hitch if the corner is waiting in trap technique.

**Mills / post-dig:** a post paired with a dig targeting a deep safety's vertical/underneath distribution. Timing and QB eyes can hold an inside help defender. Against a poach or robber that takes dig/post early, the window disappears.

**Yankee / post-over:** deep post and deep over off similar run action; often requires sturdy protection and QB anticipation. Some systems call different variations 'Yankee'; don't assume identical depths, starting receivers or progression.

**Scissors:** crossing post/corner or vertical route exchange from two receivers; force safeties or quarters defenders to exchange and maintain leverage. Can be used from stack, slot, or bunch; the exact term depends on local nomenclature.

**Switch Verticals / Switch Release:** #1 and #2 cross stems then attack vertical lanes; stress corners/safeties who use initial numbering. QB must verify whether match defenders exchange cleanly.

**Post-Wheel / Slant-Wheel:** inside breaking route complements a wheel; can exploit aggressive flat/curl leverage. Requires receiver legal crossing and wheel timing relative to help.

### 21.4 Isolation, option and man-match answers

**Choice/option:** receiver alters break based on coverage and leverage. QB must anticipate the same decision from receiver speed, body orientation and defender relationship. Misread and poor timing create interceptions because QB throws one branch while WR executes another.

**Whip/Pivot:** receiver threatens in then breaks out; useful against man inside leverage, but requires plant/footwork and space. Against zone, defender may pass off instead of chase.

**Slot fade:** receiver threatens upfield from inside leverage and widens toward space; can isolate a nickel/safety mismatch. Must preserve release space and avoid squeezed boundary.

**Double moves:** sluggo (slant-go), out-and-up, hitch-and-go, post-corner, corner-post. They exploit defender anticipation after earlier completions or tendencies. Their costs are timing, protection, route consistency and the risk of the QB predetermining a trap. They should not be treated as automatic payoff for having previously called a short route.

## 22. Teaching the QB: from a play drawing to perception-action skill

An effective QB installation defines **the same state variables** for each concept: (1) pre-snap alert, (2) protection/pressure declaration, (3) primary post-snap key, (4) QB footwork and throw schedule, (5) second/third answer, (6) escape/sack-avoidance rules, and (7) route adjustments under coverage changes. Teach with concise decision names rather than dumping every defensive label on the QB at once. 'If curl-flat widens to #2, throw stick window' is often easier to execute than 'Cover 3 except on the Sky rotation with soft trap'—provided that it correctly describes the concept and the rest of the coverage.

### 22.1 Install sequence and test battery

**Board:** point to the conflict defender and mark where the ball should arrive, not only where routes break. **Walkthrough:** line up versus correct eligibility and deceptive shells. **Indy:** QB drop, hitch and release with a defender moving at realistic timing. **Seven-on-seven:** use zone match/man/buzz variations, rotate late and change the read key. **Team:** combine the same picture with actual rush, protections and route collision traffic. **Film:** freeze *before* QB decision and ask for the rule, then unfreeze and grade the chosen response rather than outcome alone.

Some useful practice variations:

- **Same-look, different coverage:** keep a two-high shell and rotate to quarter-quarter-half, Cover 3 Buzz or man/robber. QB must confirm post-snap.
- **Same coverage, different presentation:** play Cover 3 from different corner leverage and safety shell. QB learns not to infer rules from appearances alone.
- **Hot/sim pressure:** show six possible rushers, rush four in one rep and six in another. QB identifies actual free rusher and throw window.
- **Delayed motion:** show jet, short return and RB-out. QB must re-count and communicate legally.
- **Receiver choice ambiguity:** option receiver chooses sit or pivot by leverage; grade whether QB and WR reached same solution.
- **Adverse-footwork:** first read capped while pocket begins compressing. QB hitches/climbs and moves within protection, not straight into edge rush.

### 22.2 Why quarterbacks miss throws even with a correct read

A correct read can fail through late eyes, mismatched drop depth, uneven hitch timing, insufficient anticipation, a misread receiver landmark, an unstable throwing base, or underestimation of a defender's closing path. A film grade should split **identification, decision, timing, ball placement and physical platform**. Not every open receiver should have been thrown to: the QB may have been committed to another side by progression, or the throw may have become unsafe before recognition.

## 23. Coverage stress maps: concepts versus defensive rules

The common 'play A beats Cover B' chart is useful only as a starting point. Real defenses match routes, exchange responsibilities, use brackets, disguise rotation, or pressure before deeper routes develop. The staff should instead think about **where a given concept places a decision on an individual defender** and whether help prevents the throw.

| Defensive structural behavior | Candidate offensive stresses | What could defeat those attacks? |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Static Cover 3 / three-deep | Flood, seam, dagger, spacing, backside isolation | Three-match carry, robber, trap, pressure |
| Spot Cover 2 / two-deep | Seam, smash, hole shot, middle vertical | Tampa middle runner, cloud redirect, half-safety range |
| Quarters / two-high match | Switch verticals, Mills, post-over, fast #2, RPO glance | MOD/MEG, Poach/Special, late bracket |
| Cover 1 / man free | Mesh, choice, stacks, motion, crossers | Rat/robber, banjo, elite press, free safety |
| Cover 0 pressure | Fast access, hot/sight, max protect, movement | DB leverage, trap-looking rush replacements |
| Palms / 2-read | #2-out/wheel, boundary vertical, switch releases | Corner-safety exchange executed correctly |
| Simulated pressure | Quick count, back scan, layered zone concepts | Four rushers arrive while QB misreads droppers |
| Two-high disguise | Patient post-snap read, route occupying both halves | Disguise movement delays recognition or sacks arrive |

This matrix is intentionally **not a deterministic matchup table**. Some of these calls are poor against a properly adapted defense. The practical application is to build an opponent-specific response test and hold backups if the coverage picture changes.

## 24. Situational playcalling: a different geometry on every down

**Third down** changes the defense's landmarks: defenders can protect the sticks and concede a short completion. The offensive coordinator has to know what gain is actually valuable; a five-yard quick game is not a successful third-and-eight call merely because it was completed. Conversely, a short completion on third-and-four can be an excellent answer to man pressure. Routes just short of the line to gain need realistic YAC opportunities rather than optimistic assumptions.

**Red zone** compresses vertical windows and makes man leverage, route distribution, bunch spacing and run fits more significant. On the low goal line, a route's nominal depth may physically exceed available space. The QB needs precise throw windows and a willingness to avoid forcing a ball into short, crowded geometry. **Backed up** changes risk tolerance: an intentional grounding call or sack near the goal line is catastrophic. **Two-minute** makes sideline access, play clock, spike eligibility, timeouts, and substitutions part of the concept. **Four-minute** values conversion and clock survival while avoiding turnovers.

### 24.1 A call's real utility is situational

Two plays with equal mean yards can have very different value. A safe three-yard run on second-and-three may efficiently produce first down; it may be worthless on fourth-and-seven. On third-and-ten a five-yard completion without realistic tackle-break probability is a defensive success. A shot that has a higher chance of a turnover may still be justified late in the fourth quarter when losing and needing a touchdown. Good staffs apply these decisions while acknowledging their models and opponent estimates are uncertain.

## 25. Scheme families: multiple languages for similar football problems

**Shanahan/McVay-lineage or wide-zone families** often connect condensed formations, under-center runs, shifts, split flow, boots and crossers. **West Coast language families** can emphasize precise timed routes and long call grammar. **Air Raid-derived offenses** emphasize rep-heavy pass concepts (mesh, shallow, Y Cross, four verts, stick, screens), spaced receiver rules and progression consistency. **Spread/RPO offenses** use field width, simple conflict reads, tempo and option structures. **Power/counter/gap-focused offenses** emphasize favorable double teams, pullers, insert blockers and play-action constraints. **QB-run-focused offenses** add a ball-carrier to the rushing count and stress assignment football. NFL and college systems are now frequently hybrids rather than pure categories.

A useful comparative distinction is not 'which coach runs which play,' but **where the complexity lives**: formation and motion choreography, OL rules, receiver route adjustments, QB post-snap reads, option conflict defenders, or pure repetition of a small package. A system becomes difficult to execute when these layers each introduce unfamiliar decisions at the same time. The coordinator should keep a known stable core while changing the layer that most directly attacks the opponent.

## 26. Practice, review, and a model for grading the coordinator

Create a **play-call quality log** separate from the outcome. Did the call match a practiced situation? Was there a protection answer? Did the formation elicit the targeted defensive check? Did the target defender respond as expected? Did the QB make a reasonable decision against what actually happened? Was the chosen call robust to common alternative looks? Did the offense preserve tempo and legal alignment? These questions make game planning improvable rather than mystical.

### 26.1 Suggested review fields

| Dimension | Film question | Error classes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Game plan | Was the targeted defender/rule actually stressed? | Unsupported scouting hypothesis |
| Formational presentation | Did defense adjust as expected? | Confused player count; wrong receiver numbering |
| Execution | Did players reach landmarks and handle exchange? | Route depth, handoff, missed block |
| QB | Was identification/read/timing within installed rules? | Stale pre-snap read, late release, forced ball |
| Protection | Was pocket duration adequate for the concept? | Mis-ID, loss, free rusher, QB held ball |
| Situation | Did risk and yardage fit down/distance/time? | Low-value completion, catastrophic sack |
| Adaptation | Did staff correctly interpret the previous response? | Overreaction to one outlier snap |

### 26.2 A good simulation model for offensive playcalling

For each call, store **personnel, eligible alignment, core surfaces, motion state/timing, run concept or route tree, OL blocking/protection, player assignment controllers, QB reads, alerts/checks, defensive responses observed, and decision outcome**. Simulated QBs must not magically know the coverage; coordinators must not choose from hidden defender states. A useful engine evaluates whether the formation and motion changed the *defense's perception and assignment* and whether player movement created a physically achievable lane or passing window. Use the same play with altered motion to demonstrate that any added advantage arises from an actual defender relocation or communication delay.

# PART V — FULL GAME-PLANNING AND PLAYCALLING LABORATORIES

*The laboratories are hypothetical, independently designed teaching examples. Defensive reactions are possibilities, not reconstructions of recorded NFL plays or guaranteed responses. Each laboratory separates the called play, the desired defensive behavior, the QB decision, and the fallback.*

## Laboratory 1. The week's plan versus a split-safety Tite-front defense

**Opponent model:** The defense commonly uses 4i–0–4i Tite surfaces with two deep safeties. Linebackers squeeze interior gaps while overhangs are asked to trigger against perimeter plays. Passing downs feature late rotations and four-man simulated pressures. **Personnel:** 11 and 12 packages, one mobile Y and one credible receiver running deep overs.

**Plan A:** Use 12 attached-Y wide zone and under-center split flow to threaten the perimeter. Observe whether the end squeezes the tackle, whether the overhang widens, and which safety becomes the extra run fitter. **Plan B:** Duo or pin-and-pull against the interior B-gap denial—not because those concepts automatically defeat Tite, but because they change the points of attack and double-team leverage. **Constraint:** From the same tight split and split-zone look, run boot Sail or post-over. If the defense does not chase run action, use quick spacing/choice rather than forcing an ambitious shot.

**The QB's problem:** The defense shows two-high pre-snap and drops a mugged linebacker while the edge comes. On the first deep concept, the QB checks protection and does not assume a six-man blitz merely from the alignment. After the snap he reads the actual dropping interior defender who may occupy the over lane. The fallback is a tagged back release or underneath outlet when the lane closes.

**Coaching takeaway:** This is not a 'Tite defense = run outside' equation. Every call tests a particular defender and retains an answer against the defense holding its rules. The staff should judge whether motion actually changed the force/alley geometry.

## Laboratory 2. Two calls from one look: jet motion into wide zone and counter away

**Pre-snap:** 11 personnel, reduced Z to the right and H in left slot, RB offset right. H jets toward the right. The defense may bump the weak linebacker and nickel or exchange safety support. **Call 1:** wide zone toward the motion, inviting fast flow and stressing the force defender's ability to stay square. **Call 2:** same early presentation, counter left with the backside guard and tackle pulling (a hypothetical GT installation).

**First call response:** The linebacker widens when the slot crosses, the safety rolls down, and the backside end squeezes the tackle. This suggests a later counter may catch the linebacker relocating, but the coordinator must first verify who inherited the weak-side fit. **Second call branch:** On counter away, the puller approaches a defender who wrong-arms the kick-out. The RB must recognize spill leverage and bounce or follow the wrapping insert as taught. If the defense holds the linebacker and safety instead of chasing jet, the original displacement hypothesis fails, and the coordinator should not run Counter simply because 'we set it up.'

**Film questions:** Which defender moved before the snap? Did the backside B/C gap actually become open? Was the runner's track timed to the puller? Did the motion compromise offensive legality or timing? A good simulation should show advantage only from actual changes in player states.

## Laboratory 3. Cover 1 against trips: using bunch and mesh without illegal contact

**Opponent model:** The defense uses man free with a low rat defender and checks bunch with banjo exchanges. **Offensive plan:** Begin with 3x1 normal spacing, show quick vertical access to the isolated X, then shift trips into a tight bunch and run Mesh with a vertical clearout and sit/outlet. The shift must create a legal set before a subsequent motion at the snap.

**Defensive response A — pure trail man:** The two shallow crossers generate physical traffic. One breaks behind his defender and the QB can deliver promptly. Receivers should pass close enough to create legal route congestion but must not deliberately impede defenders in a manner constituting OPI. **Response B — banjo/box:** The defense switches crossers and keeps them covered. The QB should not stare at a fake 'man beater'; he looks for the middle sit or outlet, with a checkdown contingent on the robber. **Response C — Cover 3 match disguised as man:** A trailing corner and underneath exchange can resemble man until the release declares. The QB confirms where the low hook defender goes and throws to space rather than predetermining a crosser.

**Takeaway:** Formation traffic is a *problem for defensive communication*, not a free completion. The route concept requires timing, legal execution and a defined alternative.

## Laboratory 4. A complete protection audible versus double-A mug

**Pre-snap:** 11 gun, 2x2, a six-man pass concept with the RB originally assigned to scan inside-out away from a half-slide. The defense aligns two linebackers over A gaps and both edges outside tackles. The QB must determine who is in the line's protection count and which possible rusher is the RB's responsibility; a generic call of 'blitz!' supplies no solution.

**Branch 1:** One mugged LB drops, the opposite LB comes. With the correct point and scan rule, the RB intersects the free interior path while the line passes off the four down threats. The QB works a Dagger progression, but the dropping mugger may occupy the dig window. **Branch 2:** Both muggers come and an edge drops—still a four-man rush but from an unexpected origin. The offense must sort the rush geometry rather than count helmets at the line. **Branch 3:** Six actually rush. If only six block and an assigned receiver can hot-adjust, the QB must identify whether a truly unblocked rusher remains and throw on schedule; an unsupported deeper concept becomes a poor call.

**Post-snap grading:** Separate incorrect point, late communication, lost one-on-one, RB taking wrong side, and QB holding the ball from a defensive scheme that was simply well timed.

## Laboratory 5. Quarters against 3x1: isolating a true #3 vertical read

**Offense:** 11 personnel in trips right with an isolated X left. The trips #3 threatens a vertical bender, #2 works an intermediate out, and #1 carries a deep clearout. **Objective:** Stress whether the defense uses a weak-safety Poach/Solo check, a three-over-three match, or a rotation into single-high. The QB cannot assume the route is uncovered because the pre-snap weak safety appears deep over X.

**Response A — Poach:** The weak safety poaches #3 vertical, leaving the backside corner more isolated. The QB's alert may shift toward the X matchup only if it has the leverage to win; #3 is not simply a free throw. **Response B — no Poach, LB carry:** The #3 bender may occupy a linebacker or strong safety in mismatch, but an underneath wall can distort its stem. **Response C — fast rotation:** The defense closes the middle and brackets #3, leaving a short outside access route. The QB confirms the new shell after the snap and works the installed progression.

**Coaching point:** Trips is not a numerical promise of a mismatch. The attack must be mapped to the defense's **named trips check** or to the specific defender's release rule. This is exactly where scouting a defense by its generic 'Cover 4' rate proves inadequate.

## Laboratory 6. One route family, four different quarterback read systems

**Base routes:** On the right, a deep clearout, 12–15 yard dig and short checkdown; on the left, isolated X access. **Version A — pure progression:** alert clearout if given, dig, checkdown. **Version B — weak hook key:** observe whether linebacker sinks under dig; if he does, release checkdown quickly. **Version C — safety confirmation:** post-snap MOFC/MOFO branch defines which side's window is viable before the QB uses a short progression. **Version D — isolated X alert:** if the corner cushion and help geometry satisfy a predefined rule, throw X; otherwise pivot feet to the right-side dig/checkdown.

**Teaching test:** Put the same routes on the field against a late-spinning defense. Grade the QB's *eyes and feet* and the decision moment. A QB who correctly knows the route names can still fail the play if he looks left too long then throws the deep dig late. A QB who correctly rejects the alert should not be punished because the broadcast later shows X becoming open after the decision window.

## Laboratory 7. A twelve-personnel formation carousel against base/nickel substitutions

**Personnel remains 12 throughout a sequence.** Snap 1: two attached tight ends and duo. Snap 2: Y attached, U wing, split zone. Snap 3: U motions into a detached slot, 2x2 Smash. Snap 4: U returns attached after a legal set; play-action boot. Snap 5: both tight ends detach into an empty 3x2 distribution, quick choice. These are not necessarily calls on consecutive downs: they are a package rehearsed to exploit the same defensive personnel.

**Defensive choices:** Keep base and risk a linebacker versus the detached U; substitute nickel and potentially weaken the next condensed run fit; or play a heavier coverage call with DB match rules for the TE. **Offensive constraints:** Y/U must be reliable route runners, the QB must re-count protection in empty, and the shift/motion choreography must satisfy legal alignment. **Lesson:** Personnel versatility is useful when it gives the same players *competent* answers in several spaces. A TE lined up outside who cannot threaten vertical routes may not create the hoped-for mismatch.

## Laboratory 8. Third-and-medium: a call-sheet decision against coverage and pressure

**Situation:** Third-and-5 near midfield. The call sheet lists Stick, choice, Mesh, sprint-out Flood and a six-man protection Dagger. **Film expectation:** defense likes Cover 1 Rat but can show double-A mug and three-match. **Initial QB picture:** defense packs the line with two muggers, corners at different leverage, deep safety shaded toward the star.

**Coordinator choice:** A 3x1 bunch choice/stick package with short timing and an explicit hot answer. **Branch A — Cover 1 Rat:** slot option can break away from leverage but must not drift into rat help. **Branch B — three-match:** the apex walls stick and corner traps fast flat; QB works the complementary sit/outlet rather than forcing access. **Branch C — six-man pressure:** the QB accelerates the throw and discards the deeper idea. **Alternative call:** if the defense repeatedly caps all quick windows with a low robber and drops a linebacker from the mug front, a max-protected layered concept might be preferable if practiced.

**Evaluation:** A four-yard completion with no credible YAC window fails to convert. A six-yard completion does not prove the concept was correct if the protection missed a free threat. Track the whole chain.

## Laboratory 9. Low red zone: compressed space changes coverage answers

**Situation:** First-and-goal from the seven. The offense aligns 11 bunch right with X isolated left. **Menu:** inside run, pick-safe bunch pivot, quick sprint-out, flat-to-corner and fade/stop isolation. The play caller cannot rely on a 20-yard over or a deep sail route because the back boundary severely limits depth.

**Defense:** tight man with a switch/banjo bunch check, safety leaning toward isolation. **Call:** bunch pivot with a clear-out and controlled outside route, QB taught to read defender leverage immediately. If banjo properly passes the crossing threats, the QB throws away, finds a designated outlet, or uses the installed second answer instead of holding the ball for nonexistent vertical space. A motion into bunch can force a late check, but it also risks a procedural foul or receiver arriving late.

**Coaching point:** At the goal line, receiver stem tempo, initial splits, hands versus press, and DB leverage often matter more than nominal coverage labels. Pair the pass with a run that punishes the defense for over-allocating bodies outside.

## Laboratory 10. Building a four-play constraint chain from one tight formation

**Snap family:** 12, under center, single reduced X, Y attached right and U wing left. **Base:** wide zone right. **Constraint 1:** boot left with flat H and intermediate over, punishing uncontrolled backside pursuit. **Constraint 2:** split zone with U slicing across, changing how the backside edge sees the same initial OL flow. **Counter:** tight-end trade to left and Power right/Counter left when the defense flips or overcompensates to Y strength.

**The crucial branch:** If the backside edge remains home on boot, the offense cannot force the naked. Add a sift protector or call an entirely different complementary pass. If the defense stops widening to wide zone, pressing the run track can remain effective. If safety support rotates downhill to fill, the intermediate over may become attractive, but the QB must check the actual high safety distribution and protection.

**Diagnosis:** Which first movement of the OL is identical? When does each call diverge? Did the defense react to evidence or merely to the name of the look? A physically faithful simulation should make the look alike until the actual contact/movement states separate.

## Laboratory 11. Saturdays versus Sundays: RPO, field width, and rules

**College setup:** Spread 11 personnel, ball on a wide hash, RB offset, trips field with an isolated boundary X. The offense pairs inside zone with a backside glance and a field bubble alert. Wide hash and defensive overhang distribution can create long lateral distances to adjust; the QB reads a conflict player and confirms coverage before throwing. College has its own eligibility and ineligible-downfield rules; the exact allowed OL movement must be checked under the season's NCAA code.

**NFL adaptation:** With narrower hash separation and more compressed field/boundary geometry, the corner and safety can often overlap certain routes faster. NFL ineligible-downfield enforcement places stricter practical demands on how far linemen can climb before a pass. The offense may prefer a pre-snap access throw, a very quick post-snap RPO with tightly coached line sets, or a separate run/play-action call rather than importing college timing unchanged.

**Coaching conclusion:** 'Same concept' can mean a different release, throw clock, protection, and target defender depending on level and rules. Saturday and Sunday formational labels transfer more readily than every hidden assignment rule.

## Laboratory 12. An adaptive second half: what the coordinator changes and what stays

**First-half evidence:** Wide zone averaged a modest gain because the defense squeezed the end and kept the overhang outside. Quick stick worked early, then the nickel began trapping the fast flat. The defense also stopped chasing jet motion with linebackers, using safety rotation instead. A single explosive over route hit after play-action, but on the following snap the defense poached the over from a two-high shell.

**Halftime options:** (1) Do not abandon every core run; change the RB track or insert blocker if its geometry remains sound. (2) Remove blind stick-flat throws into the trap corner; use vertical/seam stress or sit behind the trap depending on the actual coverage rule. (3) Shift from jet as a *run-fit displacement tool* to jet as a *coverage diagnostic tool*, because linebackers stopped bumping. (4) Keep play-action but tag the alternate window against the poach/robber structure. (5) Tighten the protections on concepts whose duration proved too long.

**Second-half call sequence:** secure early-down run → motion to expose safety rotation → quick access only when corner leverage gives it → repeat run from unbalanced Y surface → boot with added H protection if edge stays. Evaluate responses, not whether the preordained order looked clever. **Lesson:** Adaptation is a controlled update of a model, not throwing away an entire offense after one poor half.

# APPENDIX A — DECODING COMPLETE PLAY CALLS

A real offensive call can be much shorter or much longer than these examples, and tags mean different things across teams. The purpose of the examples is to reconstruct the **information the call must convey**, not assert an authentic NFL terminology string.

### A.1 Illustrative call: `11 / Gun Trips Right / Jet H / IZ Left + Glance X / Half-Slide Right / Kill = Stick`

**Personnel:** 11. **QB/formation:** shotgun with three receivers right and X isolated left. **Motion:** H crosses from right toward left under the proper legal motion timing. **Base action:** inside zone left; the back's track and the linemen's combination rules are called independently of the glance route. **Read:** X has a backside glance option keyed to a designated conflict player and safety access. **Protection:** the pass option must be compatible with line movement; 'Half-Slide Right' would be a separate pass variant if the team does not permit this pairing. **Kill:** if the defense shows a pre-defined overloaded picture, change to a full quick Stick call, with *all* receivers and linemen knowing what changes. **Important correction:** this illustrative string intentionally shows why a coordinator must not combine incompatible run-pass OL assignments in one opaque label. In an actual install, the run's blocking rule and the killed pass's protection rule must be separate, coherent declarations.

### A.2 Illustrative call: `12 / Ace Tight / U Trade / Wide Zone Right / Boot Left / Y Over / F Flat / X Post`

**Personnel:** 12; Y and U can become a tight surface. **Shift:** U relocates after a legal set. **Primary run look:** wide zone right. **Pass complement:** QB turns away left with a deep post, intermediate over and flat control. **Protection:** backside edge must be intentionally handled (sift, keep, naked or tagged protector). **QB read:** edge danger first, then throw schedule appropriate to the moving launch point. **Coaching note:** a play-action pass is not simultaneously the actual wide-zone handoff; the run family supplies the fake and blocking presentation. The call sheet should record two separate callable plays if both are used.

### A.3 Illustrative call: `11 / Gun Bunch Left / Switch / Six-man / Mesh Sit / X Alert Fade`

**Presentation:** three eligible receivers in a legal bunch left with a switch release. **Protection:** a sixth protector, commonly RB, adjusts the route count. **Concept:** opposite crossers with an inside settle route and a complementary vertical. **Alert:** isolated X fade only against an installed alignment/coverage trigger. **Route timing:** the switch cannot make receivers collide in a way that constitutes an illegal pick. **QB:** check protection pressure picture, evaluate isolated X alert, then work mesh/sit. **Busted call scenario:** if the defense shows six rushers and the RB leaves unexpectedly, the coordinator must know whether the hot route or protection rule replaces the back; there is no magic 'mesh beats pressure' answer.

## B.3 Film-study sequence for one opponent drive

Freeze at huddle exit and identify personnel. Freeze after offense sets and write down eligibility, splits and possible motion. Freeze immediately before snap and state pressure/coverage hypotheses. Advance to first two steps after snap: what was the safety/linebacker displacement? At QB plant, name his actual read, whether the preferred window exists, and if the pocket remains viable. At the throw, measure defender close path, route leverage and whether the release timing was on schedule. Finish by grading the *call design*, *player execution* and *outcome* separately. Repeat for adjacent calls to detect sequencing and tendency changes.

## C.1 Where the evidence stops

The exact call words, QB priorities, pressure checks, code names and route adjustment tags of an NFL team are typically private and subject to weekly change. Film can support statements like 'the corner traveled,' 'the back scanned left' and 'the safety poached #3'; it usually cannot prove the coordinator's internal terminology. The play call examples in this textbook were built to satisfy football logic, not to impersonate a club's authentic installation. Use original rulebooks for any interpretation with officiating, penalty or player-safety consequences.

## C.2 Four recommended study projects

1. Chart 50 offensive plays from one team and label personnel, actual receiver distributions, movement, protection and route family; then compare their defense responses.
2. Film-grade one QB on five repetitions of the same pass concept against distinct coverage shells. Mark first look, actual rotation, key defender, drop timing and throw decision.
3. Build a twelve-play game plan against a specific front/coverage report. For each call include what defender it is intended to manipulate and one explicit fallback.
4. Test one motion both ways in an 11-on-11 simulator with identical initial personnel and different defensive motion checks. Track actual linebacker/safety displacement, not just end results.

**Final principle:** A coordinator's real competitive advantage is not a sufficiently large list of plays. It is a well-practiced language that can present familiar football in unfamiliar ways while giving all eleven offensive players—and especially the quarterback—the same answer to a defense that refuses to cooperate.
