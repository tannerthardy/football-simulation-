# Top speed by position, NFL vs ours: are our defenders slow against the carriers? (measurement and reading; no change)

Tools/Physics, 2026-11-24, for Integration (follows the facing-law note's "0.7 yd/s lower at every angle"). Positions only; speed = the 0.2 s centred difference on the 0.1 s grid, both sides, one operator (`scripts/diag/body/top_speed.py`; tables `data/reviews/census/top-speed.md`, `vmax-by-position.md`). NFL: BDB 2025 dropbacks to the release and the completions to the end of the play; ours: the 24 census games (default engine; same files as the open census) and their completions. No game was re-run for this note.

## Answer

**Our defenders are not slow against our carriers, and against the NFL's they are faster in pursuit, not slower.** The "8.0 against 8.7" of the facing-law table is the p99 of defenders within 20 degrees of travel *in coverage, before the throw*, a throttled situation (a man in zone drifts, he does not run his top speed); it is not their capacity and it does not carry into pursuit.

| after the catch (completions, to the end of the play), p50 / p90 / p99 yd/s | NFL | ours |
|---|---|---|
| carrier WR | 5.0 / 8.0 / 9.5 | 4.6 / 8.7 / 9.7 |
| carrier TE | 5.0 / 7.5 / 8.8 | 4.6 / 7.3 / 8.8 |
| carrier RB | 4.9 / 7.5 / 9.2 | 5.2 / 8.3 / 9.3 |
| defender LB | 4.8 / 7.0 / 8.4 | 5.7 / 7.7 / 8.7 |
| defender CB | 4.4 / 6.8 / 8.9 | 6.0 / 8.3 / 9.4 |
| defender S | 5.1 / 7.2 / 8.7 | 5.9 / 8.0 / 9.3 |
| defender DL | 3.9 / 5.9 / 7.4 | 4.7 / 6.4 / 7.1 |

- **Defenders chase at a higher speed than the NFL's** (p50 +0.9 to +1.6, p99 +0.3 to +0.6 for LB / CB / S): ours run flat out after the catch more of the time, the NFL's more often are not chasing at all (pursuing from the far side, or already out of the play). Ours are not short.
- **On completions with 15+ yards after the catch** (NFL 295, ours 116; ours' median carry 26 yd against 20, so the sets are not matched): the mean of the three fastest defenders' p90 speeds over the carry is **7.25 against the carrier's 8.05 in the NFL (ratio 0.91; a defender's p90 beats the carrier's on 56% of plays)** and **8.32 against 8.40 in ours (ratio 0.99; 68%)**. So our first defenders are *closer to* the carrier's speed than the NFL's are, not further. "Outrun on a fair angle" in Defense's tables (19-36% against 12%) is therefore not a top-speed deficit of the bodies; it is the angle, the take-off or the path (a pursuit controller or contact result), which is Defense's measurement to read with this table in hand.
- **Before the throw** (dropbacks, open movement, p99): WR 9.0 | 8.5, TE 8.2 | 7.8, RB 7.9 | 7.4, DL 7.0 | 6.5, LB 7.2 | 7.0, CB 7.9 | 7.8, S 7.2 | 6.9. Ours is 0.1-0.6 lower in coverage and routes, receivers and linemen a little more than defenders (the NFL's top receivers run a stem to full speed on go routes; ours throttle by `ROUTE_EFFORT`'s family constants), so the gap between defenders and receivers in the dropback sample is the same as the NFL's (DB about 0.9 under WR in both, p99).
- **Time to reach 90% of the group's p99 from 3 yd/s** (median, dropbacks): WR 1.6 | 1.4, TE 1.3 | 1.4, RB 1.1 | 1.3, LB 1.0 | 1.0, CB 1.3 | 1.7, S 1.2 | 1.4, DL 0.9 | 0.5 (small, ours n 979). The sets of starts that reach 90% of p99 are a small minority on both sides (a dropback rarely lets a man run flat out), so these are indications; CB and S take 0.2-0.4 s longer in ours to get there.

## From the code: how vmax is made

`derivePhysical` (`src/players/physical.ts`): `solveSprint(tenSplit, forty)` fits V and tau to the 10-yd split and the forty, and `inPadsSprint` multiplies V by `gameSpeed = clamp(0.95 - 0.0008 x max(0, weight - 195 lb), 0.84, 0.95)` (a stated in-pads reduction: 0.95 up to 195 lb, 0.87 at 295 lb, 0.84 floor) and tau by `gameSpeed x GAME_BURST (2.2)`. There is **no position term and no side-of-ball term**: a defender and a receiver of the same forty and weight have the same vmax. What differs by position is the generator's forty and weight, and the resulting generated means (`vmax-by-position.md`, 8 teams): WR 9.83, CB 9.84, S 9.67, RB 9.56, LB 9.22, TE 8.99, QB 9.11, edge 8.91, DT 7.84, OT 7.69, OG 7.58, C 7.69 yd/s. So CB equals WR (the NFL's table says the same: DB 8.7, WR 8.7) and S is 0.16 under; LB sits 0.6 under WR. **Our defenders are not short of our receivers by more than the NFL's gap.** The p99 speeds in play are lower than vmax (WR 9.83 against 9.0-9.7 in play) because vmax is a limit the sprint curve approaches, the endurance fade (`enduranceFactor`, 10% after 3.5 s) and the effort throttles act on it, and a short run never reaches it; the in-game gap to vmax is a few percent on both sides.

What this does not show (Integration's question was the body, not the controller): (1) whether a pursuer's *path* takes a fair angle; (2) the NFL's pursuit sample is mixed with plays where the defender is not trying to catch the man; (3) vmax's in-pads factor is a stated choice (not fitted to the NFL's gaps) and the NFL's own p99s here are tracking-limited (the 257 yd/s maximum of one NFL CB row is a tracking glitch; the p99 is robust to it).

## Caveats

- The after-catch sets are small and not matched on carry (NFL carries 20 yd median, ours 26); the ratio is a median across plays, not an average over comparable carries. The direction (ours not slower) holds across the position rows, the difference in size should not be relied on.
- Speeds are smoothed the same on both sides but the NFL's provider smoothing is not ours; a smoothed track lowers peaks, so the NFL's p99 is, if anything, a lower bound on its real top speed. That would argue the real gap is larger in the other direction than shown (ours faster in pursuit by less than the table says), not that ours is short.
