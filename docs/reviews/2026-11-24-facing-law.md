# Speed and acceleration against the angle between orientation and travel: NFL vs ours (measurement, then the law; no build)

Tools/Physics, 2026-11-24, for Integration (the facing-as-state item: `tools/body-measure` 2026-11-18, YAW_CAP #862, DIR_START #850, Rules' slant_flat trace). Positions and the tracked orientation only. NFL: BDB 2025 weeks 1-9 dropbacks, snap to release, 2.1 million player-frames of open movement (nobody within 1.5 yd, speed 1.5 yd/s and over), `o` = the provider's orientation, travel heading and speed from the 0.2 s centred difference of position, acceleration along travel from the speed series, yaw rate of `o`. Ours: 16 games (seeds 101, 105, 109, 113 x 4; engine-91, develop 0226f0d3), hips + trunk twist = shoulders as `o`, the same code on both sides (`scripts/diag/body/facing_law.py`, `facing-sim.mts`). Tables with n: `data/reviews/census/facing-law.md`.

## What the NFL's bodies do

Top speed (p99) as a share of the same group's speed within 20 degrees of travel, by the angle between orientation and travel (0-20 / 20-45 / 45-75 / 75-105 / 105-135 / 135-165 / 165-180), and the forward acceleration capacity (p90 of the acceleration along travel for frames moving 1.5-4 yd/s) as a share of the 0-20 degree value:

| group | | 0-20 | 20-45 | 45-75 | 75-105 | 105-135 | 135-165 | 165-180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| defensive backs, NFL | top speed | 1.00 (8.7 yd/s) | .96 | .85 | .74 | .67 | .64 | .63 |
| defensive backs, ours | top speed | 1.00 (8.0) | .90 | .83 | .73 | .71 | .64 | .59 |
| linebackers, NFL | top speed | 1.00 (7.9) | .91 | .82 | .73 | .65 | .61 | .60 |
| linebackers, ours | top speed | 1.00 (7.1) | .93 | .92 | .78 | .69 | .62 | .60 |
| defensive backs, NFL | forward acceleration | 1.00 (+4.6 yd/s²) | 1.00 | .99 | .95 | .85 | .73 | .66 |
| defensive backs, ours | forward acceleration | 1.00 (+5.0) | .97 | 1.07 | 1.04 | .90 | .82 | .75 |
| linebackers, NFL | forward acceleration | 1.00 (+5.4) | .98 | .89 | .79 | .70 | .59 | .53 |
| linebackers, ours | forward acceleration | 1.00 (+5.8) | .79 | .78 | .76 | .67 | .58 | .55 |

- **Defenders:** top speed falls with the angle between the body and the travel as a line from full speed within 20 degrees to about 0.6 at a backpedal (side-on, 75-105 degrees, 0.73-0.75; backpedal 0.60-0.63). Ours is on the same curve to within 0.05 at every angle (DIR_CAP, calibrated on exactly this), and about 0.7 yd/s lower at every angle including the straight-ahead one (8.0 against 8.7, 7.1 against 7.9), which is a top-speed offset of the bodies, not an angle law. The forward acceleration falls later and less than the speed cap: flat to 75 degrees, then .95, .85, .73, .66 for defensive backs; ours is higher than the NFL's by .05-.09 from 75 to 180 degrees for backs (1.04 / .90 / .82 / .75), so the legs in our model push a little too hard turned sideways or back. At running speeds (3-7 yd/s) the acceleration percentiles by angle are close: defensive backs side-on at 5-7 yd/s +1.1 / +2.5 (p50 / p90) against ours +1.1 / +2.1; backpedalling (165-180 at 3-5) +0.8 / +2.3 against +1.2 / +2.6.
- **Receivers are not constrained by the tracked orientation, and cannot be tested with it.** Top speed (p99) by angle, NFL: 8.7, 8.8, 8.7 (45-75), 7.8 (75-105), 7.1, 8.2, 8.6 (165-180); forward acceleration +6.3, +6.2, +6.1, +5.8, +4.6, +4.5, +5.6. A receiver whose tracked orientation is 135-180 degrees off his travel runs as fast as one facing forward: `o` is the upper body (shoulders), which counter-rotates and looks back while the legs run on. **The tracking cannot tell the hips' angle from the shoulders'**, so it neither supports nor refutes "a body lets a man run full speed with his hips 60 degrees off": the NFL's receivers also run full speed (5-7 yd/s, 8.7 p99) with their shoulders 45-75 degrees off (13% of frames). What it does show is that a speed cap read off the tracked orientation would slow NFL receivers that run at full speed; the cap in DIR_CAP is read off the hips and the trunk is priced separately (TRUNK_PRICE), which is the right split.
- **Yaw (3-7 yd/s):** NFL yaw rate of `o` p50 / p90 / p99: receivers 37 / 120 / 262 deg/s, defensive backs 28 / 87 / 205, linebackers 27 / 81 / 181. Ours: receivers 18 / 179 / 464, backs 9 / 142 / 300, linebackers 16 / 216 / 288. Ours moves in jumps (a lower median, a heavier tail), as the 2026-11-18 note found; the NFL's is smoothed by the 10 Hz tracking (a floor on the real rate, not a ceiling), so the tail comparison is a lower bound on how jumpy ours is relative to the real thing and the median comparison is the more trustworthy: ours turns 2-3 times less often through small angles and then flips.
- **How long it takes to turn the hips:** the tracking cannot give it (smoothed orientation, no hip joint); the yaw-rate percentiles are what exists: at the NFL's p90 (80-120 deg/s) a 90 degree change takes 0.75-1.1 s of sustained turning, and 180 degrees about twice that; the engine's `turnRate` (4.5-12 rad/s, 258-688 deg/s) turns the hips 90 degrees in 0.13-0.35 s. Whether real hips turn that fast in a flip, the tracking does not say.

## What the law should be

- **One law for facing against travel that every position shares:** the capacity of the legs along the travel direction is multiplied by `c(theta)`, theta the angle between the hips and the travel, with **two curves from the same angle**: the **top speed** (the cap) falls linearly from 1 within 20 degrees to about 0.62 at 130 degrees and beyond (DIR_CAP, already right for defenders to 0.05), and the **forward acceleration** (the start and sprint force) stays at 1 to 75 degrees and then falls to about 0.65-0.7 at 180 degrees (defensive backs .95 / .85 / .73 / .66 at 90 / 120 / 150 / 180 degrees; linebackers lower, .79 side-on and .53 at 180, ours already at .76 / .55). A single parameter set, no table by position.
- **What it replaces:** DIR_START's `L = 0.67` start penalty and its `sin²` form become the acceleration curve (at 90 degrees it would give 0.95 for backs rather than 0.67, which agrees with the NFL's running acceleration but not with DIR_START's 0.66-0.70 lateral/forward *start speed* at 0.2 s; those are different quantities: a start from rest is limited by the push direction against the pelvis in the first steps, a running acceleration by the force-velocity curve, and the evidence here is for the second; the two should not be conflated until a start-speed table by angle is built on the same operator). The backpedal cap is the 180-degree end of the speed curve (0.60-0.63), so it needs no separate constant. `YAW_CAP`'s purpose (slow the jumpy hips) is the third piece and is separate: it fixes the yaw tail (ours p90 142-216 deg/s against the NFL's 81-120), not the capacity.
- **What the measurement cannot support:** (1) anything about receivers' hips (the tracked orientation is the shoulders, and the sim's `o` here includes the trunk twist, so the receiver rows compare shoulders to shoulders but not legs); (2) the time to turn the hips (smoothed orientation); (3) a causal reading: the NFL's off-axis frames are defenders in coverage turning for a reason (backpedalling, flipping, carrying a man) and the speeds they show are what they chose, not the most they can do; the p99 is a capacity proxy, not a measured capacity; (4) the start-from-rest quantity, which needs the start operator of #850, not these running frames.
- **What it says about Rules' trace:** the body's cap by the *hips'* angle is not violated by a receiver whose hips are 55-70 degrees off (the cap there is about 0.85); Rules' receiver does run 6.2-6.4 yd/s where the NFL's flat runners run 5.2-5.3, but the NFL's receivers at 45-75 degrees of shoulder offset also run as fast as straight ones, so the slower NFL flat runners are not explained by an orientation-speed law; the cut-speed and start measurements point at the route's requested speed (the through speed and the get-off drive share), not at a facing cap.

## The look-stage flip

Found and fixed in draft PR #883 (`tools/look-side-flip`): `sideOf`'s raw sign flips at +-180 degrees when the head is within 0.2 rad of straight, so `lookJoints` (facing = heading + sign(d) x over) and the head's clamp to +-neck swung to the other shoulder between ticks. The side is held per man until the target is within 130 degrees of straight ahead again; a head clearly turned over 0.2 rad still decides.

## Addendum (2026-11-24, after Integration's two inputs): the run-linebacker shed case, and what the law does and does not claim

**Integration's correction, recorded.** "One body fault under several findings" does not hold as stated. DIR_CAP's top-speed curve is within 0.05 of the NFL's at every angle for defenders; the forward acceleration the legs give is higher than the NFL's by 0.05-0.09 at 75-180 degrees for backs; and the tracked orientation (`o` = shoulders) cannot test receivers' hips at all. This note proposes one law and builds nothing; it does not claim that facing is the fault behind the flat runner or behind the linebacker's shed.

**Run Game's shed case (n 62: speed 1.55 / 2.04 / 2.38 yd/s at +0.05 / +0.25 / +0.50 s, facing 65 / 52 / 31 degrees off the command; NFL linebackers leave a block at 3.2 yd/s).** The question was whether a facing penalty on *acceleration* explains it, against the flat runner whose hips are 55-70 degrees off and who runs 6.2-6.4 yd/s. It does not, and the two cases do not need two laws:

1. **In the code the facing acts on the speed cap, not on the start force.** `locomotionAccel` (`kinematics.ts`): `full = start x directionalSpeedFactor(facing vs command) x trunkPrice`, `cap = full x effort`; the push toward the cap is the sprint curve `(cap - speed)/tau` (or `lift` at low speed, where the start force is `vmax x fat` and does not depend on facing: the comment there records "tracked NFL pullers reach 0.5 yd/s sideways at 0.1 s"). At 65 degrees off the command the cap is 0.84 of vmax, about 6.6 yd/s for a linebacker: **it cannot bind at 2 yd/s.** At 5-7 yd/s a flat runner with hips 55-70 degrees off has the same cap (about 0.85 vmax, 7.5-8.4 yd/s): it does not bind at 6.2-6.4 either. One law, two cases, neither limited by it.
2. **Lone body in the World** (`scripts/diag/body/facing-start-bench.mts`, a 112 kg linebacker, vmax 7.9, command full speed, no contact): starting at 0.3 / 1 / 2 yd/s with the hips 0 / 30 / 65 / 90 degrees off the command, the speed at +0.5 s is the same to 0.05 yd/s whatever the hips (2.77 / 3.24 / 3.91 yd/s with the velocity along the command); the hips square to the command within 0.2 s (61 degrees off at +0.05 s, 8 at +0.25 s, 0 at +0.5 s for a 65 degree start). The mean acceleration is 4.5-4.9 yd/s² from rest. **A lone body's facing costs no acceleration.** What does cost: velocity across the command, because it has to be turned (starting at 1 yd/s moving 90 degrees across the command the speed at +0.5 s is 2.66 against 3.24, mean 3.3 against 4.5 yd/s²; at 2 yd/s it is 2.91 against 3.91).
3. **The real shed, traced** (`shed-trace.mts`, Run Game's `shed-recovery.mts` extended, on `run/tackle-repair` 057932182, 4 games, 219 releases, 45 linebackers; `scripts/diag/body/shed-trace.mts`): per tick after the engagement ends the linebacker **plans** 5.3 yd/s² along his command (command 9.0 yd/s, effort 1.0, drive full, availability 1.00, no push, not `free`: 5.3 / 5.3 / 4.6 at +0.05 / +0.25 / +0.5 s), his support is balanced and the brace and felt load are small (brace 9 against a plan force of 560), and the controller **sets an explicit facing** (the hips are not following his travel) that is 66 / 50 / 31 degrees off the command. What he **realizes**: on ticks with no contact and no hand joint (354 of 405) 4.6 yd/s² along the command against 5.2 planned (88%; 4.4-5.8 by how far his velocity is off the command); on ticks in contact with someone (35, 9%) 2.1 against 5.4 (39%); with a joint still on him (19) about nothing. His velocity at +0.05 s is 1.9 yd/s and **48-55 degrees off the command** (the median of the 45; 17 of them 60 degrees or more, where the speed does not grow at all, 1.37 -> 1.23 yd/s over the next 0.45 s). So the slow shed is (a) release velocity that is mostly across where he wants to go, which has to be turned, with the friction ellipse's lateral half (`lateralEnvelope`) the limit on how fast, (b) the minority of ticks in which he is still touched or held, and (c) a release speed of 1.9 yd/s against the NFL's 3.2 *after* 0.5 s. It is not balance (support deviation 0.02-0.04, availability 1.00) and it is not a facing penalty on acceleration (none exists, and the cap is not close).
4. **What follows for the law.** The proposed single law (cap by hips-off-travel, acceleration flat to 75 degrees) covers both cases, and neither is a test of it. The shed case belongs to what the controller commands and where the body is when the engagement releases him (his explicit facing, his release velocity, the contacts after it): Run Game's trace and Defense's post-shed controller. I could not compare the NFL's release velocity direction (the tracking does not mark an engagement's end), so "the NFL's linebackers leave with a smaller sideways component" is a hypothesis, not a measurement.

**The same trace for the linemen (n 141 in the 4 games; `shed-trace.mts`; Defense's case: SHED_PURSUE's full request facing his travel leaves the speed at 2.13 -> 2.15 yd/s at +0.5 s).** Not a facing law, and not a state left over from the engagement either: (1) the controller's explicit facing is 93-95 degrees off the command at every tick to +0.5 s (the hips are not following the travel and are not asked to), and velocity is 90 degrees off the command at the release (42 at +0.25 s); the facing cap at 93 degrees is 0.73-0.75 of vmax (about 5.5 yd/s), which does not bind at 2-2.6 yd/s. (2) Plan along the command 4.6-4.9 yd/s², availability 1.00 at every tick, effort 1. (3) **He is held or touched again:** a hand or other joint is on him on 12% of ticks in 0-0.15 s, 18% in 0.15-0.3 s and **34% in 0.3-0.55 s** (13% of all ticks have him in a new engagement, `d.engagement` set), and on ticks in contact the achieved acceleration along the command is 0.9 against 2.6 planned, with a joint -1.0 against -1.5 (he is being pulled back). On the ticks with no contact and no joint he realizes 4.2 yd/s² of 5.2 planned (81%). The linemen's slowness at +0.5 s is therefore a second block or a re-grip (a double team's second man, a lineman re-engaging), not a body state; whether the NFL's linemen are re-engaged as often at the same moment is a measurement for Defense's operator (the tracking cannot tell). The leftover support state is small where it was looked at: `sup.lean` 14 on free ticks against 201 with a joint, brace 11 and 130, availability 1.00 throughout; feet lock was not logged.
