# Pass protection: base, anchor, the bull rush and falls: sources and what they mean for the engine

Reviewed 2026-11-13 for the Fable sim agent, Integration and Flex. Input is a set of
coaching sources (some the user pointed at directly: the Wisconsin Football Coaches
Association article, Newsome, Bentley, the Iske manual) and a pasted research summary
that cites more. Companion to [Trenches](trenches.md) and the
[offensive line protection textbook](../references/offensive_line_protection_textbook.pdf).
Nothing here changes the engine.

**Bottom line.** The sources agree with what the engine's own diagnosis already
found, and add a short list of specific mechanisms and numbers. The central pass-pro
rule across all of them is *do not lean on the rusher*: keep weight back and under the
hips, feet before hands, let the hands and a sunk base do the work. Our loaded-falls
review reached the same place ("every pass blocker asks for full lean into his rusher,
where coaching keeps weight back"). The engine already has a capture-point support
model (Hof's idea), a lean and posture state, and an experimental anchor re-set, so the
new pieces are narrower than the pasted summary implies. See "What the engine already
has" before proposing anything.

## 1. What each source teaches

### WFCA, Engstrand (high school): the anchor-hop drill

The article the user marked. Wauwatosa West HS, a team moving from Power-T to spread;
two drills, written for coaches. Level: high school, one coach's method; his claim that
the drills took the team from last to second in conference passing is not evidence for
the technique.

- **Drill 1, four stages** ("push to move"): initial set off the inside leg with chopping
  feet and hands protecting the chest; add two kicks; add one post (answer to an inside
  move: get the inside leg "in front"); add a second post and an **anchor hop** (answer
  to the final move, a bull rush). Shoulders and hips end square to the line with only
  the head turned to the target. Carry hands high to protect the chestplate.
- **Drill 2, Anchor Hop-Refit.** Offense starts compromised on purpose (high pad level,
  no hip bend, outside and on-top hands); defender starts with the better position and
  bull rushes at 50%. The blocker must **hop backward and drop his hips under the
  defender's level at the same time**; while he hops, the defender **refits his hands
  one at a time** to a winning position (under and inside); then the blocker lifts the
  chestplate upward with force while **walking the defender back toward the line**.
- **Success criteria:** the defender's force goes **upward rather than straight ahead**;
  blocker's back has a negative arch, pads and hips lower than the defender's, hands
  inside and underneath the defender's, shoulders over hips over feet.
- **Why not chop.** The coach says the hand chop is the easy fix most linemen reach for
  and is less effective: it leaves the chestplate unprotected so the defender can push
  harder, and does not change pad level, so the blocker is pushed back into the QB.
  Maintain a hand on the defender at all times; contact controls the tempo of the rush.

### Iske manual (college, Southwestern Oklahoma State, 2018)

- **Stance (Ch. 2):** feet about armpit width: close enough that the first step gains
  width, wide enough that the knees stay inside the ankles. Stagger: outside toe even
  with the instep of the inside foot, more for longer players. Toes slightly outside the
  ankles so weight sits on the insteps; heels down. Down hand barely grazes the turf
  (weight in the feet). **More weight on the foot opposite the direction of travel**
  ("butt shift") so there is no false step, in any stance, run or pass. "Load the
  spring": hips pushed back about six inches, back arched, core tight. Elbows "stapled"
  to the ribs. Tackles on obvious passing downs may put weight on the toe with the foot
  parallel to the sideline, which makes it nearly impossible to move the inside foot
  first, so it is not allowed all the time.
- **Pass fundamentals (Ch. 12):** eyes up and open (the most under-coached item); aim at
  the center of the chest if manned, the near knee of the most dangerous threat in gap
  protection. Cover up, get nose to nose, stay square: "if a defender can grab your
  shoulder you are in trouble." Hands low, strike on the rise (hips roll on contact);
  high hands give the defender a target and force a pushing track. **Feet tight to the
  ground ("mow the grass"), both feet down at contact as much as possible**, steps over
  bags never taught. **Feet before hands.** **Keep a center of balance between the
  feet**; tilt the shoulders opposite the set direction. Kick set aims at the inside
  shoulder point and reads three things: chest ("if he gives you chest, take chest":
  two-hand lifting punch and grab; if driven backwards, **hop back with both feet and lower the hips**), shoulder (speed: two-hand punch the shoulder and
  retract; do not duck for the chest) or across the face (turn it into a post). Centers
  and guards own the depth of the pocket and **give ground grudgingly**; tackles own the
  width. Vary sets.
- **Errors (Ch. 17):** over-setting from steps that are too big; **two small steps of
  6-8 in beat one big step**; drag the second step; steps mirror each other or the
  shoulders turn. Bull rush trouble is pad level, a set that is not explosive, a late
  punch: "sprawl the feet, toes outside the ankles, knees inside". Against a rusher
  who feels you leaning: **push-pull (grab the chest and pull while hopping back with both
  feet), close to the line so the rusher is not thrown into the QB's legs**; a leaning
  blocker gets spun or push-pulled. Counters: wrist grab, long arm (underneath punch at
  the elbow lifts the arm and the rusher's pad level; a chop turns the shoulders), club
  (lock out the arm on that side), spin (spin with the spinner, or half-spin and box
  out), hand slapper (be patient, "punch and off").
- **Set menu:** kick, jump, short, high hands, fake punch, donkey punch, stab and
  swipe, boxer; do not set the same way on 25-40 dropbacks.
- **Movement (Ch. 18):** react with the foot nearest the slant, keep the stagger, drag
  the second step. Pass off twists, stay on level with the partner, do not pass a twist
  off to air. Gap protection: the tackle identifies an edge-pressure threat from the
  defenders outside the box; a rolling safety during the cadence is a tell.

### Newsome (high school, 2008): the geometry of the feet

Five keys: win the set, relative position, body position, lateral movement, separation.
Feet shoulder width or slightly wider, toe to instep, weight on the insteps; two-step
set by the rusher's alignment; set to **"the spot"** (rusher's inside shoulder line meets
the QB's launch point), which Newsome calls the most important key; shoulders square
(turning them makes it a run block). Tackle: inside leg up, outside leg back, outside
leg splits the crotch; guard: nose to nose, gives little ground. "Bar stool" posture;
head never past the inside knee. Kick step **8-12 in**, ball of the ankle drives into
the ground so he lands on the instep; the post foot is dragged only as far as the
kicker moved; weight to the outside of the foot means loss of balance and getting
beaten inside; **feet together ("slooping") and excess air time invite the bull rush**;
parallel feet are easy to pull. Hands low (thumbs near the bottom of the numbers);
punch is a snap, not a wind-up. Pocket is a half-moon with 4-5 yd of cushion. No
percentages, no degrees; stagger is qualitative.

### Bentley, USA Football (former Pro Bowl center, 2016): three bull-rush rules

Set the feet with confidence (any hesitation exposes you to straight-line pressure);
**do not lock out the arms** (fully extended arms push your center of mass away from
your body and cut stability); **do not drop your backside** (past about 90 degrees of
knee flexion the center of mass sits too far behind: "sinking your hips" is not
"sitting in a chair that is pulled away"). No hand-placement, base-width or anchor
numbers. This disagrees with SWBU and Iske on lockout (below).

### Joe Thomas, SI (NFL tackle, 2015)

Three sets: quick, **angle** (a wider drop to about 45 degrees: if beaten the rusher
"still has to go another four yards" before a sack, against "two more" after a vertical
set) and vertical; the choice depends on the protection and the drop, and an angle set
needs a guard or center sliding with him or it exposes him to twists. Against power
he **sinks his hips and keeps re-sinking to hold roughly a 90-degree angle, or a
straightened angle gets him "peeled over backwards"**, and uses a **backward "hop"**
to plant and blunt the rush. "99.9% of the time" it is leverage, angles, hand placement
and hips, not strength; a blocker with poor leverage uses about half his strength while
the rusher with good leverage uses all of his.

### Southwest Baptist OL manual (college, Blake, 2008)

Eight teaching steps (stance, squat, shuffle, target, jam, timing the jam, set, alignment
reads). **60/40 weight "over the front and back two points of contact" in a two-point
stance**, which the manual does not explain further; **do not read it as a measured NFL
distribution, and I cannot tell which foot carries 60.** Squat: hip-wide base or slightly
wider, toe-to-heel stagger, knees bent not the waist, bowed back. Shuffle: a power step
and a kick slide, one foot always on the ground, never cross the feet, "base travels
with the hips and shoulders." The pasted summary says it "describes a squat-and-pop
technique." It does not: "Squat & Pop" is a one-line glossary entry ("regain leverage
and control when at a disadvantage during a bull rush"). Sled drill: "lock out after
punch."

### Thorn, USA Football (2018): snatch-and-trap

A downward one- or two-handed swipe at the rusher's wrist, forearm or elbow to **remove
the contact the rusher is leaning on**, so his weight falls forward. Used against rushers
with their weight too far forward (often in a long arm or bull rush), ideally before
contact or as the counter to a long arm after a jump set. No foot or hip mechanics
described; failure cases not discussed; no numbers.

## 2. Where the sources agree, and where they do not

Agree: weight stays back and under the hips; feet before hands; keep both feet on the
ground as much as possible; stay square; keep the hips down; do not reach; small steps;
eyes open; the rusher's body gives the read; vary the set; give ground in a controlled
way.

Coaching disagreements the engine should leave as the player's choice, with the cost
coming from physics. (Correction 2026-11-13: an earlier version of this table set Iske
against the hop. He teaches it, as the answer to being driven back; the real disagreement
is how freely to use it.)

| Question | One side | Other side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lock out the arms? | Bentley: no, it moves the center of mass away and cuts stability | Iske: after stopping the charge, lock out the elbows for separation. SWBU: lock out after the punch |
| Hop? | WFCA, Thomas, **and Iske (Ch. 13: "if you are being driven backwards, hop back with both feet and lower hips to stop his momentum"; push-pull also hops back)** | Newsome: feet low, excess air time invites the bull rush; Iske's general rule is both feet down as much as possible, so the hop is the exception he teaches when carried back |
| High or low hands | Iske: low, strike on the rise; high hands give a target | Iske also teaches **high hands and the fake punch as a change-up** to bait a hand slapper |
| Punch and grab vs punch and off | Against a bull rusher grab within his frame | Against a hand slapper, punch and off to keep weight back |
| Chop | WFCA: less effective than the hop | Iske: legitimate escape if beaten inside; swipe turns the shoulders |
| Stance weight | Iske: weight in the feet, hand barely down; opposite foot to the step | SWBU: 60/40 two-point; tackles may toe-load on passing downs (Iske) |

## 3. Forces and balance science: what is established and what is not

The pasted summary's own conclusion holds: **there is no published NFL measurement of the
force a tackle resists across a live 1-2 second pass-block engagement.** What exists:

| Study | Verified result | What it cannot tell us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gatt et al. 1997 (5 Division I-A linemen hitting an instrumented sled) | Impact force **3,013 +/- 598 N**; estimated L4-5 peak compression **8,679 +/- 1,965 N**, anterior-posterior shear 3,304 +/- 1,116 N, lateral shear 1,709 +/- 411 N | An impact into a sled, not a sustained push; the spinal loads are **internal joint loads**, not forces on a rusher. Do not use 3,013 N as an anchor strength |
| Knudsen & Andersen 2017 (11 amateur players, staggered-stance starts, 5 m sprints) | Peak horizontal ground force **800.6-861.7 N** across three starts (normal start fastest, 1.77 s; backward false step slowest, 2.01 s, with the lowest mean force) | Accelerating own mass from a stance, indoors, non-cleats. The pasted summary dates it 2018; the paper is 2017 |
| Lambach et al. 2015 (15 NCAA linemen, 121 kg) | Peak knee loading in blocking drills at flexion **above 70 degrees** (walk and jog below 44) | Unloaded movements; peak loads were higher in jogging |
| Riley et al. 2013 (9 pro-squad players) | Joint ranges in combine drills approach physiological limits | Not blocking |
| van Mierlo et al., PLOS ONE 2023 (10 healthy adults, 23.6 years, 70 kg) | In a staggered stance (0.15 m wide, 0.40 m long) with pelvis pushes of 3-12% body weight for **150 ms** in 8 directions, pushes **perpendicular** to the line between the feet needed a recovery step in **over 60% of trials at 12%** and took about **0.8 s** to return against **0.4-0.5 s** for pushes along it; the trailing leg carried **60.3%** of weight | Young non-athletes, brief pushes, arms crossed, a stance unlike a lineman's. Use the **direction dependence**, not the numbers |
| Hof 2005 | The extrapolated center of mass, **XcoM = x + v/omega0, omega0 = sqrt(g/l)**; stability is the distance of XcoM from the edge of the base of support | A theory of standing and walking |
| Hof & Curtze 2016 | Allowing for the time recovery takes, the **effective** base of support after sudden pushes was **about 30% of the static area** | Standing perturbations; relevance to a blocker's feet is by analogy |

Not verified: the 2001 sled study's r = 0.03 between bench press and sled displacement
(403). The pasted summary's caution still stands as a principle: do not model the anchor
as mass times strength.

Order-of-magnitude check, not validation: the engine's lineman push capacity is about
1.0-1.07 times body weight with traction capped near 1.1 (loaded-falls review). The
sprint-start peak horizontal force above is about 0.98 times the subjects' body weight
(861.7 N for 89.4 kg). Same order, different task.

**Time-based protection evidence.** PFF (Jahnke, 2014, 2013 data) judged blockers by
cutting each play at 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0 s: nearly every tackle is near-perfect at 2.0 s,
separation appears at 2.5 s, and at 3.0 s the best tackle's pass-blocking efficiency
was 87 and samples are small. NFL Next Gen Stats reports an average **time to pressure of
2.9 s**, an average rusher pressure rate of **10.3%**, "quick pressure" within 2.5 s and a
75% model-probability pressure threshold, from proprietary, unvalidated models. Both
are outcome-and-time curves; neither measures contact force. Our NFL operators
(`docs/reviews/nfl-sim-operators.md`, the edge-rush review) already compare timing
and positions on tracking.

**Falls.** The pasted summary found **no published rate** of linemen falling on run versus
pass plays and warns against inventing one. Agree. Our own census
(`docs/reviews/loaded-falls.md`) counts the engine's falls (OL 1.7 per 100 run player-plays,
4.4-5.2 on passes) and says explicitly that there is no NFL label to compare to. The
suggested route (a modest manually coded film sample: who falls, intentional or not, lost
assignment or not) is the right one.

## 4. What the engine already has (develop `516d8729`)

Read from code and the in-house reviews; nothing run.

- **A capture-point support model** (`src/physics/support.ts`): per body, a linear
  inverted pendulum. `xi` is the capture point's deviation from the region his gait
  controls; inside the base it decays, beyond it gravity diverges it against a recovery
  stepping speed, and past base + step/omega0 he falls. This is Hof's XcoM idea; omega0 =
  sqrt(g/h), pad level lowers the center of mass. Receiving a load he was leaning on that
  disappears displaces `xi`, which is the physics of snatch-and-trap and push-pull.
- **Lean and posture:** a committed `lean` that follows the felt load (up to +25%),
  posture (pad level) 0-1, `feetLock`, a `tipAccel` reduced pitch/roll term.
- **Base is one scalar radius** `baseRadius = 0.11 + 0.05 x balance + 0.04 x mass term`
  yd, scaled by `0.75 + 0.5 x posture`: **isotropic**. It is the region his gait
  controls, not a foot spread. There is a `VirtualFoot` per body, explicitly "not an
  articulated foot".
- **Pass-pro flags in `engagement.ts`:** `PASS_ANCHOR` (the pass set asks for an anchor,
  lean growth 0; its source comment says off but the code reads it on unless set to `0`,
  and `docs/reviews/2026-11-10-lean-anchor-scorecard.md` compared it with `LEAN_PITCH`
  at 0 and 1: check its current state before relying on either),
  `PASS_SINK` (off), `TACKLE_SET` (set depth from the called drop, off), `TACKLE_BRAKE`
  (off), **`TACKLE_RESET` (off): the anchor's base re-set**: when carried faster than he
  asked to retreat and pressed past what his base holds, a short replacement step, "of
  which a hop is one form", feet light during it (no braking, no drive; a stand-in because
  "the model has no per-foot normal load; debt"), he sinks, and on landing his lean is
  set to his base limit. It cites the in-repo OL protection textbook ("stop retreat, bring
  feet under hips, anchor").
- **Rusher moves** (`agent.ts`): bull, long arm, speed, dip, rip, swim, club, spin,
  snatch, shed, with a chosen approach and `RUSH_HANDS` for the long arm.
- **Known problem, in-house:** the loaded-falls review finds a bull-rush stalemate leans
  two linemen on each other at about body weight (capture point about 0.95 yd ahead of
  the feet against a recovery limit of about 1.1), so any change of load (a spin, a
  release, a bump from his own back) drops him, and traces it to every pass blocker
  asking for full lean (`push: 1`) where coaching keeps weight back.
- **The edge-rush review (2026-11-12):** the tackle never stops kicking, ends deeper than
  the QB (-8.2 vs -7.8 at 3.0 s; NFL tackle -6.8, QB -7.8), and the rusher wins in front of
  him rather than around him. `TACKLE_SET/BRAKE/RESET` were written for that.

## 5. What the sources add: candidates, in the order I would look at them

Each is a **candidate**: name the decision it could change before building it, keep
controller changes (what he asks for, on what he can see) apart from physics changes
(what the body achieves), and do not add a capacity to rescue a number.

1. **Lean discipline in the pass set (controller).** Every source says weight stays back
   and the hands, sunk hips and feet do the work, and a leaning blocker is the one who
   gets push-pulled or spun (Iske). This is the in-house diagnosis again, with coached
   wording to build from: ask for the lean the rusher's load needs, not a fixed full lean;
   feet before hands; do not reach. Check what `PASS_ANCHOR` already does and how
   falls behave with it on and off on the same snaps.
2. **The anchor hop as a decision with a cost (controller + the existing re-set).**
   WFCA and Thomas give the trigger (a bull rush walking him back), the action (hop
   back and drop the hips beneath the rusher's level at once, re-fit hands inside and
   underneath), and the success criteria (the rusher's force no longer straight ahead,
   the blocker's pads and hips below his, hands inside, then walk him back).
   `TACKLE_RESET` models the step; the drill's criteria give a witness list: a bull rush
   against a blocker with a good and a poor starting position, 50% and full effort, the
   chop alternative (which the source says costs a bare chest and pad level), and the
   mirrored side. Iske and Newsome argue the other way (air time invites the bull
   rush): both costs are physical, so leave the choice to the blocker.
3. **"Redirect the force upward" needs a vertical or normal-load term (physics, debt).**
   The WFCA success criterion is that the rusher's drive goes upward. The re-set's own
   comment records that there is no per-foot normal load. Without it, lifting the rusher
   cannot reduce his traction or his push; with it the whole lift, long-arm-underneath
   and chestplate-lift family would have a mechanism. **Do not build it unless a named
   decision or witness needs it.**
4. **A base that depends on direction (physics, modest).** PLOS gives only the principle:
   a push along the line between the feet is easier to recover from than one
   perpendicular to it. Our base is isotropic. A shoulder-width, staggered stance has a
   short axis and a long axis; stagger "toe to instep" versus "toe to heel", width
   "armpit" versus wider, parallel versus staggered feet ("parallel feet make a lineman
   easy to pull", Newsome) are all coached geometry. A two-parameter ellipse oriented to
   his facing would carry it without foot models (anatomical feet are paused research).
   Hof and Curtze suggest the effective region is smaller than the geometric one:
   check whether `baseRadius` is meant as one before widening anything.
5. **A cost to sitting too low (physics, check).** Bentley (past about 90 degrees of
   knee flexion the center of mass is too far behind), Newsome ("bar stool"), Thomas (hold
   roughly 90 degrees, re-sink) all describe an optimum, not "lower is always better".
   In our support model a lower posture raises omega0 and widens the base, monotonically.
   Find out whether something stops "lowest posture" being free (drive, stepping speed,
   the gait's pad-level speed cost) rather than capping it.
6. **Both sides exploit the other's lean (decision on each side).** The support model
   already makes a vanished load displace `xi`. What I did not find: a blocker choosing
   a snatch-and-trap or a push-pull against a rusher who is leaning, or the reverse, as an
   action; and a blocker reading a **chest versus shoulder versus inside** cue to choose
   the response (the coached rule in Ch. 12). Grep found none in `src/sim`, so treat
   it as unconfirmed: the pass-block owner knows. These are coached rules from observation
   of the rusher's body, which fits our rule that cues must be observable.
7. **Set depth and who owns it (controller).** Centers and guards start the fight at the
   line and give ground grudgingly; tackles own width (Iske); an angle set gives the QB
   room but needs a sliding guard (Thomas); the set aims at a point from the call (Newsome's
   "spot"). The edge-rush review's finding (tackle deeper than the QB) is the same gap, and
   `TACKLE_SET/BRAKE` are the experiments. The sources support the direction and add
   position-specific roles.
8. **Step lengths as coaching inputs, not calibrations.** Kick step 8-12 in (Newsome); two
   steps of 6-8 in (Iske); mirrored steps; weight on the opposite foot. These are
   prescriptions at high-school and college level. They suit a set-step request; they
   are not measured NFL foot placement.

## 6. Validation the sources suggest

- Matched bull rushes on the same saved snaps at different push angles and effort,
  tracking blocker-rusher separation, depth lost, whether the blocker stays in front of
  the rusher, the capture-point margin, and falls: guards separately from tackles (depth
  versus width). The matched-restage tool
  (`scripts/diag/matched-restage.ts`) supports shifted replays with a dice noise floor.
- Hop trigger and recovery from the WFCA success criteria.
- A manually coded film sample for falls (see section 3).
- Protection-survival by time to throw against PFF's and NGS's time horizons, as context.

## 7. What not to do

- Do not use 3,013 N (impact) or 8,679 N (spinal load) as a sustained anchor strength.
- Do not build "mass times strength"; the sled study that said bench press explained
  almost nothing is unverified here, but the principle (leverage, posture, feet) is in
  every source.
- Do not hard-code one technique: lockout, hop and chop each have a coached opponent.
- Do not give anyone a fall probability or roll a fall from a rating. Falls come from the
  support model's lost balance.
- Do not read the 60/40 figure, the 6-8 and 8-12 inch steps or the stance widths as
  measured NFL values.
- Do not treat the pasted summary's recommendations as a plan without the
  "what the engine already has" section: the capture-point model, lean, posture and a
  re-set step exist.
