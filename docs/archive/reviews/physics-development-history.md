## 1. The philosophy

**Football skills are different ways of controlling the same physical body.**

The physics engine decides what is possible: mass, momentum, friction, reach,
balance, grip, and the forces that legs and hands can deliver. Motor control and
football intelligence decide how well a player uses those possibilities.

Take tackling. An elite tackler and a poor tackler have the same kind of body
under the same physics. The elite tackler does better because he:

- gets his body into a better position;
- applies force at better angles;
- times his hands;
- keeps leverage and balance;
- chooses correctly between anchoring and redirecting;
- recovers efficiently when he misses.

The poor tackler's weaknesses show up as worse choices and worse execution of
those same controls. They are never a lower tackle-success number.

What this means for code:

- **No arbitrary final outcome overrides.** Do not replace actual contact with
  "the tackle succeeds with probability p" or "the block wins if rating A >
  rating B". Disclosed execution variation may affect perception, placement,
  timing or grip acquisition before the result. It cannot bypass reach, force,
  movement or shared effort limits.
- **Ratings map to control, not results.** A rating may change reaction delay,
  aim error, hand timing, posture, step frequency or force limits. It must not
  scale a win chance or multiply yards.
- **Few interpretable parameters.** Name the physical or control quantity,
  effective response law and plausible range. Prefer measurements, and allow
  calibration of identifiable movement/recovery parameters against matched
  data. Label assumptions where observations cannot identify the detail.
  An anatomical mechanism is not required to justify a reduced response law.
- **No timers pretending to be physics.** A block lasts as long as the hands
  hold and the feet keep the base. It does not last "0.8 s".
  Finite response, placement and get-up times may be declared effective laws;
  they must not arbitrarily award a block win or terminate a valid contact.
- **One world.** Every body moves through `src/physics/world.ts`. A technique
  works by sending intents (feet, hands, grasp, lunge) into it. A technique
  never moves bodies directly.

A useful check for any change: *could two players with identical bodies get
different results only because one controls his body better?* If yes, the change
fits. If the result depends on a number that was set to produce that result, it
does not.

## 2. The test suite

The suite has five layers. They run from cheap and exact to expensive and noisy.
Each layer has its own job; do not use a noisy layer to answer a question a
cheaper one can.

| Layer | What it checks | Where | Cost | When to run |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contracts | Momentum accounted for by ground/external impulses, no unexplained passive energy, bounded penetration, finite state, symmetry and declared numerical tolerances; exact analytic checks where applicable | `test/world-physics.test.ts`, `test/grasp-world.test.ts`, `test/physics.test.ts` | seconds | changes affecting these mechanisms |
| 2. Scenarios | The owner's six interactions (redirect, squeeze, release, seal, approach tackle, side hit), run with the real techniques. Asserts the contracts plus coarse physical directions: a harder hit displaces more, both sides of the field match | `test/scenario-pack.test.ts`; full report from `scripts/scenario-pack.ts` | seconds | every change |
| 3. Technique regressions | Pursuit, tackle and pass-contact behavior that was diagnosed and fixed before | `test/pursuit-contact.test.ts`, `test/pass-contact.test.ts` | seconds | every change |
| 4. Probes and convergence | Repeatable measurements, not pass/fail: interaction readiness, results across substep counts (6/12/24/48), labs that isolate one interaction | `scripts/interaction-readiness.ts`, `scripts/diag/pack-converge.ts`, `scripts/open-field-lab.ts`, `scripts/tackle-lab.ts`, `scripts/trench-lab.ts`, `scripts/diag/*` | a minute or two | when you change a mechanism, before committing |
| 5. Real-world comparison | Speed, acceleration, get-off and yards before contact against NFL tracking summaries; whole-game calibration | `scripts/kinematics-compare.ts`, `scripts/calibrate.ts` | minutes; games are noisy (about ±3 points over 8 games) | before committing a change that moves players; as a guard, not a tuning target |

`npm test` runs layers 1–3 together with the rest of the project's tests. Cost
varies by source and host; the latest recorded 476-test checkpoint took 76.71 s.
Test counts do not establish football readiness. Write probe output to the ignored
`out/` folder. Commit only summaries you have reviewed, under
`data/reviews/physics-world/`.

**Weight the layers by how directly they show the physics is right.** A failing
contract is a bug, always. A scenario that looks wrong is very likely a bug. A
tracking mismatch is strong evidence. A game statistic (points, punts, drives)
is weak evidence about the physics, because play calling, clock and coverage all
feed into it. Never tune physics to move a game total.

## 3. How to make a physics change

1. **Start from an observation.** Choose one thing that looks physically wrong,
   in a scenario, a trace or a tracking comparison. Write down what a real
   player's body would do instead.
2. **Trace it before fixing it.** Find which force, joint or intent produced
   the result. Determine whether coupling, control, a reduced law or missing
   state explains the failure before adding physical detail.
3. **Fix the cause in the physics or in the control.** Decide which one it is:
   - something the body *cannot* do, or does in a way no body could, belongs in
     the world (`src/physics/`);
   - a poor choice belongs in control (techniques and behaviors).
   Do not add an outcome adjustment downstream to cover either.
4. **Add the cheapest test that would have caught it.** Prefer a contract or a
   small scenario. One test per bug is usually enough.
5. **Run the layers in order.** Run `npm test` and `npm run typecheck`. Run the
   probe or lab for the area you changed, and its convergence check if the
   change touches contact. Then compare tracking. Run games last, only as a
   guard.
6. **Record the evidence.** Update the readiness doc with what changed, the
   before/after numbers, and what is still wrong. Regressions belong in the doc;
   do not hide them.

Change one mechanism at a time. Stop if success depends on compensating,
unidentifiable strength changes. Calibrating a small set of identifiable
movement/control parameters is legitimate; fitting forces to game totals is not.

## 4. How much testing is enough

Tests cost something. Each one adds run time, maintenance, and a bit of
resistance to future correct changes. A test is worth keeping when it catches
real mistakes cheaply and fails only for real reasons.

**Worth it (high benefit, low cost):**

- Tests for accounting, force limits, symmetry, finite state and bounded
  penetration. Use exact analytic assertions where justified. General contact
  refinement uses declared tolerances and fall margins over football intervals,
  not bit-identical full trajectories at every resolution.
- One or two scenario tests per owner interaction, asserting directions
  ("harder hit, more displacement") rather than exact values.
- A regression test for each bug that was hard to find.

**Usually not worth it:**

- Exact football outcomes ("this tackle gains 2.3 yards"). These break on every
  correct improvement, and they freeze the current tuning in place.
- Tests that repeat a contract already checked at a lower layer.
- Statistical pass/fail tests on whole games. They are slow and flaky, and they
  pull the work toward tuning. Keep games as a measurement you read.
- Very large parameter sweeps in the default suite. Run them as probes when
  you need them.

**Too much testing** looks like this:

- `npm test` takes more than about 2 minutes, so people stop running it.
- A correct physics fix requires editing many test thresholds.
- Tests describe the current numbers instead of the physical requirement.

When that happens, delete or loosen tests rather than adding more. A test that
fails for a correct change is a cost, not a safety net.

**Too little testing** looks like this:

- A mechanism has no conservation or symmetry check.
- A bug comes back after it was fixed.
- Nobody can say whether a change made the physics better or worse without
  running full games.

## 5. Tests worth adding next

Add each test together with the physics work it guards, not in bulk. The groups
are ranked by value. Within a group, the first items matter most.

Most of these share one shape: **paired runs**. Run the same setup twice with
the same seed, and change exactly one thing, such as a control input, mass,
speed or angle. Assert the *direction* of the difference, over a small sweep of
a few cases rather than one point. Paired directional tests are cheap, have
little noise, and survive correct tuning changes.

### A. Skill is control (tests the philosophy directly)

The same two bodies run each rep. Only the control inputs differ. The better
controller should win more often, by a clear margin. Each should also stay
inside the body's limits.

1. **Tackle:** better approach angle, breakdown speed, hand timing and pad
   level, against worse ones. Expect more tackles made, closer to the line of
   contact, and fewer misses.
2. **Block and anchor:** inside hand placement and a wide, low base, against
   outside hands and a narrow, high base, when facing a bull rush. Expect less
   ground given.
3. **Pass-rush bend:** lean and hip angle on a speed rush around the same
   tackle. Expect a tighter path to the quarterback's spot, and more falls
   when the lean is overdone.
4. **Carrier cut:** plant timing and lowering the hips before a cut, against
   cutting tall. Expect more separation from the same pursuer.
5. **Recovery:** after the same missed tackle, a better controller is back in
   pursuit sooner.

Also add the **ceiling check**. Run a maximally rated controller with
randomized intents. It must never exceed the body's speed, acceleration,
grip or force limits. This catches control code that bypasses the physics.

### B. Physics cannot be cheated (cheap, exact, run every time)

1. **Order independence:** shuffle the order of the bodies in the world and
   compare within declared solver/trajectory tolerances. Reject systematic
   team or role advantage from row order. Do not require floating-point identity
   for every finite iterative multi-contact solve.
2. **Determinism:** the same seed gives an identical play, tick for tick.
3. **Random-intent fuzzing:** run random bodies, from very light to very heavy,
   with random intents for a few hundred ticks. The contracts must hold:
   momentum accounting, bounded overlap, finite state, no energy created.
4. **Translation and swap invariance:** the same rep at a different yard line
   gives the same result away from boundaries. Swapping which team is offense
   in a symmetric rep swaps the result.
5. **At-rest stability:** standing players and a settled pile stay still. No
   creep, no jitter, and energy decays.

### C. Physical scaling laws (paired, directional)

1. **Mass:** a heavier carrier at the same speed carries further after first
   contact. A heavier hitter displaces more.
2. **Speed:** a faster hit moves more momentum. A faster carrier is harder to
   stop with an arm.
3. **Angle:** a head-on hit stops forward progress more than a glancing one at
   the same speed. A hit from behind adds to the carrier's motion.
4. **Grip location:** a wrap at the body holds a heavy back where an arm grab
   at full reach slips (`grasp-world` covers part of this).
5. **Leverage:** once contact height exists, the lower man wins at equal mass
   and effort.

### D. Multi-body football reps (scenario layer)

Add these to the scenario pack as each mechanic becomes physical. Assert the
contracts, mirror symmetry and one coarse direction each.

1. **Double team:** two blockers move a tackle further than one, in the
   combined direction. Splitting the double needs a real force or angle
   advantage.
2. **Gang tackle:** a second tackler stops forward progress sooner, and pile
   momentum is fully accounted for.
3. **Fall forward:** a carrier stopped upright still falls forward for a short
   distance, from his own momentum.
4. **Stiff arm:** a hand on the tackler's shoulder keeps distance and costs the
   carrier some speed, through the same hand-joint physics blocks use.
5. **Sideline:** a carrier hit near the sideline is carried out by momentum,
   not by a rule.
6. **Bull rush into the pocket:** depth over time against a set anchor, read
   against NFL pocket-push tracking.

### E. Real-world anchors (probes first, tests only after they match)

1. **Free-runner forecast:** start from an NFL tracking frame and simulate one
   second for players who are not in contact. Compare the predicted and actual
   positions. This tests movement physics with no play calling involved. Keep
   it as a probe because it needs raw data.
2. **One-on-one open-field rates:** tackle, miss and broken-tackle rates, and
   yards after contact, from the open-field lab, read against NFL rates.
3. **Tracking bands:** speed and acceleration percentiles by position, DL
   get-off, yards before contact. Add a loose band test only once the value
   matches. A test that locks in a wrong value is worse than none.

### Also: convergence gates

Turn the substep convergence probe into a test with a tolerance for each
interaction. The redirect scenario does not yet converge (closest approach
3.24 → 2.13 yd from 6 to 48 substeps). Add its gate once it is fixed.

### What not to add

- Exact yards or win percentages for any rep.
- Whole-game statistics as pass/fail tests.
- A new test that repeats an existing contract with different numbers.
- Tests for mechanics that do not exist yet. Write the test together with the
  mechanic.

## 6. Quick reminders

- Physics first. No coverage, play pool or pressure work until the owner gives
  an explicit OK on the physics.
- The test count is not readiness. The gates in the readiness doc are.
- Keep `npm test` fast. Put slow measurements in scripts.
- Use real units (kg, yd, s) and cite a source for every physical magnitude
  you introduce.
- If you are unsure whether something is physics or control, ask whether a
  better player with the same body could do it differently. If he could, it is
  control.
